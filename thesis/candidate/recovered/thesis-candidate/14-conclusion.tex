\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Is the HKO body a local maximum of the systolic ratio among convex bodies?
Our main theorem gives a partial answer: it is a local maximum among
ten-facet polytopes. Every sufficiently
nearby ten-facet polytope has ratio at most $(3+\sqrt5)/5$, and equality occurs precisely
under translations, positive dilations and linear symplectic maps. The
restriction to ten facets is essential to the coordinate neighborhood used
in the proof.

The argument also gives a useful way to work at a singular optimization
problem. One can continue feasible choices and use their values as upper
bounds, without continuing stationary optimizers. At HKO, finitely many
such bounds suffice: after the symmetry directions are removed, their
derivatives force a strict decrease in every direction. The algebraic
calculation establishes the finite conditions, while the geometric proof
identifies the upper bounds and relates their coordinates to nearby bodies.

The empirical work approached the problem from another direction. It used
geometric measurements and standard data-science methods to look for patterns
that might lead back to mathematical explanations. We found patterns below
systolic ratio one and confirmed some of their usefulness on fresh samples,
but did not establish an extension to ratios above one. A geometric
calculation expresses the ridge-area
measurement on products through edge lengths and widths of the factors.
For rotated regular pentagons, equation~\ref{eq:ds-pentagon-ridge-capacity}
proves an exact inverse-square relation between this descriptor and the
systolic ratio. This explains the association on that restricted family,
not on the sampled population as a whole. The exploratory samples produced
no new ratio above one.

We implemented, optimized and extended two methods for computing capacity:
the Haim--Kislev quadratic program and a flow graph using the local passage
geometry of Chaidez and Hutchings.
The expanded variational proof explains why the first problem is finite;
the second method gives an alternative for cross-checking computations.
For products of planar polygons, we proved reductions that make the finite
search smaller. A short analytic proof gives the capacity throughout the relative-rotation
family of two regular pentagons. The matrix argument in
Section~\ref{sec:pentagon-affine} extends the formula to independently
linearly deformed regular pentagons and proves that HKO attains the sharp
systolic bound throughout this larger family. The original exhaustive
SageMath rotation proof remains available as computational provenance.
A separate product-position argument shows that every symplectic product
of two full-dimensional planar convex bodies admits a capacity-increasing
rigid rotation, although the Lagrangian endpoint can have smaller capacity.
The relative placement of the factor planes thus supplies another geometric
direction to investigate.

Two directions remain particularly close to the questions studied here.
First, the local theorem could be tested against deformations that change
the facet count. Adding or splitting facets leaves the forty-dimensional
chart used in the proof, so it requires a new argument. Second, the
statistical associations invite geometric interpretation. A useful next
conjecture would explain an association on a clearly specified family and
survive attempts to break it by changing the sampling distribution.

The results therefore give a local theorem around the known counterexample,
finite methods for investigating nearby and sampled polytopes, and measured
successes and failures of several search ideas. The larger question of how
capacity and volume constrain one another remains open beyond these families
and neighborhoods.
