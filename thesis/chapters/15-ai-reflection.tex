\section{AI in the research process}
\label{sec:use-of-ai}

As the agents became more capable, my work shifted from directing individual
steps toward specifying what the finished work had to accomplish. They could
develop a substantial mathematical argument from an incomplete suggestion,
yet sometimes needed repeated intervention to pursue a much more ordinary
objective. My main difficulty increasingly concerned what they treated as
success. This section gives my interpretation of that experience; the separate
disclosure records the extent and provenance of AI use in the thesis.

\subsection{A mathematical objective with room for discovery}

The local maximality proof illustrates how much could be delegated without
specifying a procedure. I asked a GPT-5-series model to develop a local
maximality argument for HKO in facet coordinates. I suggested looking for
several upper bounds and somehow showing that together they control a
neighborhood after quotienting out the symmetries. I had neither specified a
finite certificate nor worked out how to construct the upper bounds.

The model developed essentially the entire argument from that starting
point. It identified a sufficient configuration of twenty-six upper bounds
in the twenty-five transverse dimensions. An initial derivative-based
approach had provided only twenty-five nonsingular branches, too few for the
certificate. The model then used the weights $\beta$ in the quadratic
objective to construct feasible sections: an invertible $5\times5$ closure
minor lets five weights depend smoothly on the facet parameters while the
remaining weights stay fixed. This also supplied upper bounds from singular
optimizer sets that the initial approach could not treat. The model completed
proof steps involving the volume and wrote the SageMath code for the exact
computations.

\subsection{A pursued criterion of success}

Other tasks produced a more puzzling contrast. An agent could reason
competently about a problem and still stop after a first step, answer a
related question, or keep preparing work that it had been asked to finish.
These failures sometimes looked oddly motivational: the next useful action
seemed to be within the agent's capabilities, but its behavior was directed
toward a different stopping point. An agent could correctly describe my goal
and still pursue something else. I found it useful to distinguish understanding
a goal from choosing the criterion of success that directs the work.

My hypothesis is that training with verifiable rewards encourages models to
learn a concept of a \emph{Grader}: a representation of what will judge the
task completed successfully. Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards
uses outcomes that can be checked, such as a mathematical answer or a
program's performance on test cases. For example, DeepSeek-R1-Zero was
trained with rule-based accuracy and format rewards; its reasoning strategies
were not supplied as complete human demonstrations~\cite{aiGuo2025}.
I suspect that such training can teach a model to adopt a criterion and
organize its work around satisfying it. The training result motivates this
interpretation, but does not establish that it explains the behavior of the
agents I used.

This interpretation concerns more than reward hacking or literal obedience
to an instruction. An agent need not exploit a loophole in a stated test, or
misread a particular word, to pursue the wrong objective. A plan for improving
a chapter can be a good answer to a conversational turn while leaving the
requested chapter unwritten. Likewise, a useful preliminary calculation can
become the apparent endpoint of a task that required a theorem. Each answer
may be sensible on its own. The failure is that the criterion organizing the
work covers only part of the intended result.

The practical requirement is sufficient overlap: satisfying the criterion an
agent pursues must also produce a useful end result for me. The goals need
not coincide. A clear objective makes completion evaluable: what result is
needed, why it is needed, which constraints matter, and what would leave the
task unfinished. It does not require prescribing
every intermediate action. This way of specifying the HKO task was effective
because the mathematical objective constrained the result while allowing the
agent to invent the method. I found it useful to think of prompts as both cueing
learned behavior through their wording and supplying context for deliberate
reasoning.

\subsection{Keeping the whole task in view}

Long conversations made the distinction between a useful answer and a
completed task increasingly important. At checkpoints during such a task, I
want to ask whether the current reasoning, evidence, and actions are advancing
the intended goal as a whole within the available budget. This question should
expose a change of destination: delivering only a first step, solving an easier
related problem, or replacing completion by repeated planning. The same
question belongs at the end. A successful evaluation of several components
does not answer whether the requested result has been delivered.

The budget is part of that judgment. An investigation can produce useful
information and still be a poor choice if it consumes the time needed to
finish the work. When the task is to deliver a readable chapter, identifying
another possible improvement has to be weighed against writing and revising
the actual chapter. When the task is a mathematical search, the value of
another computation depends on the question it can resolve and the resources
left for following up. Progress has to be assessed against the intended
outcome and its constraints, rather than the amount of activity generated.

This does not call for inspecting every step. An autonomous agent should
choose and revise many of its own intermediate steps. The important human
intervention is often to restore the purpose that those steps serve. More
detailed instructions can help when a constraint was missing, but can also
obscure the goal beneath local requirements. I increasingly needed to explain
the result I wanted and the context that made it useful, then judge whether
the work still addressed that result.

\subsection{Why exposition remained difficult}

For the mathematics required by this MSc project, I often found the agents'
capabilities sufficient to make substantial progress with little detailed
direction. Producing passable mathematical exposition was much harder to
delegate. This was surprising to me given the amount of written material
used in language-model training. The later work on the thesis became a
prolonged struggle with writing and task-management quirks, even when the
models could partly recognize those quirks in discussion.

One recurring problem was prose that presented itself as polished before it
communicated its content clearly. Inflated claims of significance, colorful
phrasing, and abstract summaries could make a passage sound complete while
leaving its mathematical point difficult to recover. This is what I mean by
\emph{AI slop} in the present context. An agent's ability to decipher a dense
paragraph did not show that a reader could comfortably follow it. The same
problem arose with forward and backward references across several pages.
An agent with those pages in context could follow the references, while a
human reader had to remember deferred points or interrupt the argument to
recover an earlier definition.

I suspect that preference training contributes to this tendency. In
reinforcement learning from human feedback, rankings of model outputs can
be used to train the model toward preferred responses, as in
InstructGPT~\cite{aiOuyang2022}. If an impressive presentation is easier to
recognize in a brief comparison than a clear explanation is to assess
through sustained reading, that preference signal may favor the appearance
of quality. This is my hypothesis about a possible source of the prose I
encountered, not a demonstrated causal account of those models' training.

The practical difficulty persisted even after it had been named. A model
could acknowledge that a passage needed a concrete argument and then return
another description of the argument's intended role. It could recognize
that the goal was a readable chapter while spending its effort on further
diagnosis. I had to devise corrections tailored to recurring behaviors;
there was no single instruction whose recognition reliably produced the
desired writing. Understanding a criticism in one exchange and carrying it
through a sustained piece of work proved to be different capabilities.

This experience also limits the lesson I draw from the mathematical success.
The proof work in this project was tractable enough for the available models
that their quirks often mattered less there. The difficulty of exposition
was not captured by the formal sophistication of its subject. Choosing the
right explanation, maintaining its purpose across a chapter, and removing
language that obscured the mathematics remained demanding tasks.
