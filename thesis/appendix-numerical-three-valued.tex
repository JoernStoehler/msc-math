% Three-valued predicate relaxation for finite-precision arithmetic.
% Translates each exact Boolean predicate in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ehz}
% into a three-valued version (TRUE / FALSE / UNKNOWN).
%
% Status: draft, not yet Jörn-reviewed.

The exact algorithm (Algorithm~\ref{alg:ehz}) relies on
Boolean predicates---adjacency tests, positivity checks,
rank conditions---that cannot be evaluated exactly in
floating-point arithmetic.
We replace each Boolean predicate~$P$ with a three-valued
predicate~$\tilde P$ taking values in
$\{\textsc{true}, \textsc{false}, \textsc{unknown}\}$,
subject to the \emph{soundness requirement}:
\[
  P \text{ true in exact arithmetic}
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  \tilde P \neq \textsc{false},
  \qquad
  P \text{ false in exact arithmetic}
  \;\Longrightarrow\;
  \tilde P \neq \textsc{true}.
\]
In other words, $\textsc{true}$ and $\textsc{false}$ are
reliable verdicts; $\textsc{unknown}$ means the numerical
evidence is inconclusive.

\paragraph{Impact on implications.}
The exact algorithm uses implications
$P \Rightarrow Q$ (e.g.\ ``if adjacent, include'') and
equivalences $P \Leftrightarrow Q$.
Under three-valued evaluation, each such implication
$\tilde P \Rightarrow \tilde Q$ has a $3 \times 3$ truth
table.
The key entries are:
if $\tilde P = \textsc{true}$ then
$\tilde Q \in \{\textsc{true}, \textsc{unknown}\}$
(never $\textsc{false}$, by soundness);
if $\tilde P = \textsc{false}$ then $\tilde Q$ can be anything;
if $\tilde P = \textsc{unknown}$ then $\tilde Q$ can be anything.

\paragraph{Impact on the algorithm's control flow.}
The exact algorithm has two kinds of decision points:
\begin{description}
\item[Inclusion decisions] (``unless $P$, skip''):
  The exact rule ``if $\lnot P$, skip $(S, \sigma)$''
  becomes ``if $\tilde P = \textsc{false}$, skip.''
  When $\tilde P = \textsc{unknown}$, the pair is kept
  (conservatively), since the exact~$P$ might be true.
  This means the numerical algorithm searches at least as
  broadly as the exact algorithm.
\item[Value tracking] (``record $Q$ if admissible''):
  An admissibility predicate $A(\beta)$ (e.g.\ $\beta_i > 0$
  for all~$i$) becomes three-valued.
  The algorithm tracks:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item the best~$Q$ where $\tilde A = \textsc{true}$
    (certified value),
  \item all~$Q$ values where $\tilde A = \textsc{unknown}$
    (uncertain candidates),
  \item nothing where $\tilde A = \textsc{false}$
    (provably inadmissible, discarded).
  \end{itemize}
  The certified value is a reliable lower bound on the exact
  maximum.
  The uncertain candidates may or may not be admissible;
  they are passed to the error tracking
  (Section~\ref{app:error-tracking}).
\end{description}

We now describe the three-valued version of each predicate
in the algorithm.

\paragraph{Adjacency (search-space reduction).}
The adjacency pruning
(Corollary~\ref{cor:adjacency-pruning})
discards pairs where consecutive facets are non-adjacent.
Adjacency is determined combinatorially from the polytope's
vertex--facet incidence: two facets are adjacent if and only if
they share at least one vertex.
Vertex--facet incidence is tested by checking
$\langle n_i, v \rangle = h_i$ (up to a tolerance), so the
three-valued version is:
\begin{align*}
  \tilde P_{\text{adj}}(i,j) &=
  \begin{cases}
    \textsc{true}
      & \text{if some vertex } v \text{ satisfies }
        |\langle n_i, v \rangle - h_i| < \varepsilon
        \text{ and }
        |\langle n_j, v \rangle - h_j| < \varepsilon, \\
    \textsc{false}
      & \text{if } \min_v \max\bigl(
        |\langle n_i, v \rangle - h_i|,\;
        |\langle n_j, v \rangle - h_j|\bigr)
        > \varepsilon', \\
    \textsc{unknown}
      & \text{otherwise (some vertex is near the boundary
        of incidence for at least one facet).}
  \end{cases}
\end{align*}
The algorithm skips $(S, \sigma)$ only if
$\tilde P_{\text{adj}} = \textsc{false}$ for some
consecutive pair.
When $\tilde P_{\text{adj}} = \textsc{unknown}$, the pair
is kept conservatively.
In practice, for polytopes with vertices of moderate norm
($\|v\| \lesssim 100$) and heights $h_i \in [0.1, 10]$,
the inner products $\langle n_i, v \rangle$ are well-separated
from~$h_i$ for non-incident vertices, so the $\textsc{unknown}$
case rarely arises.

\paragraph{Positivity filter (admissibility).}
The filter in Step~3 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:ehz} requires
$\beta_i > 0$ for all $i \in S$.
The computed~$\hat\beta$ differs from the true~$\beta^*$ by a
rounding error that depends on the condition number of the
KKT system.
The three-valued version is:
\[
  \tilde P_{>0}(\hat\beta_i) =
  \begin{cases}
    \textsc{true}
      & \text{if } \hat\beta_i > \varepsilon_\beta, \\
    \textsc{false}
      & \text{if } \hat\beta_i < -\varepsilon_\beta, \\
    \textsc{unknown}
      & \text{if } |\hat\beta_i| \leq \varepsilon_\beta,
  \end{cases}
\]
where $\varepsilon_\beta$ is an error bound for the solve.
Three sub-cases explain the $\textsc{unknown}$ verdict:
\begin{itemize}
\item If $0 < \beta^*_i < \varepsilon_\beta$:
  the pair is genuinely admissible but may be incorrectly
  discarded ($\hat\beta_i$ could round negative).
\item If $-\varepsilon_\beta < \beta^*_i < 0$:
  the pair is genuinely inadmissible but may be incorrectly
  kept ($\hat\beta_i$ could round positive).
\item If $\beta^*_i = 0$:
  the pair is covered by a smaller
  pair $(S \setminus \{i\}, \sigma|_{S \setminus \{i\}})$,
  which the algorithm also examines.
\end{itemize}
\noindent
If $\tilde P_{>0} = \textsc{true}$ for all~$i$, the candidate
is certified admissible and its~$Q$ value is tracked as
certified.
If $\tilde P_{>0} = \textsc{false}$ for any~$i$, the
candidate is discarded.
If $\tilde P_{>0} = \textsc{unknown}$ for some~$i$ (and no
$\textsc{false}$), the candidate is tracked as uncertain
(Section~\ref{app:error-tracking}).

\paragraph{Near-singular dismissal.}
This predicate has opposite polarity: $\textsc{true}$ means
``safe to dismiss'' (an exclusion of $(S,\sigma)$ from further
consideration), while the previous predicates use
$\textsc{true}$ to mean ``keep.''
Lemma~\ref{lem:rank-deficiency-dismissal} allows dismissing
rank-deficient systems, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:approximate-dismissal} extends this to
near-singular systems.
The SVD of the KKT matrix provides the smallest singular
value~$\sigma_{\min}$ and the associated right singular
vector~$(\delta\beta, \delta\lambda, \delta\nu)$.
The three-valued version checks whether $\|\delta\beta\|$
is large enough to apply the proposition:
\[
  \tilde P_{\text{dismiss}} =
  \begin{cases}
    \textsc{true}
      & \text{if } \sigma_m \text{ is small and }
        \|\delta\beta\| > \sigma_m / \sigma_C
        \text{ (proposition applies),} \\
    \textsc{false}
      & \text{if } \sigma_m \text{ is not small
        (system well-conditioned),} \\
    \textsc{unknown}
      & \text{if } \sigma_m \text{ is small but }
        \|\delta\beta\| \leq \sigma_m / \sigma_C.
  \end{cases}
\]
The algorithm dismisses $(S,\sigma)$ only when
$\tilde P_{\text{dismiss}} = \textsc{true}$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:dismissal-error-bound} bounds the error).
When $\tilde P_{\text{dismiss}} = \textsc{unknown}$,
the near-null direction is confined to the multiplier
block~$(\delta\lambda, \delta\nu)$:
the $\beta$-component of the solution is approximately unique,
and the algorithm uses it directly, flagging the candidate as
uncertain (Section~\ref{app:error-tracking}).
When $\tilde P_{\text{dismiss}} = \textsc{false}$,
the system is well-conditioned and $\beta_0$ is used directly.

\begin{remark}[Parameter scales in practice]\label{rem:parameter-scales}
For polytopes generated by our random sampling
(Section~\ref{sec:random-sweep}),
the relevant quantities have the following typical magnitudes:
the symplectic inner products
$\omega_0(n_i, n_j) \in [-1, 1]$ (unit normals),
with $\omega_0(n_i, n_j) \approx 0$ occurring primarily
for near-Lagrangian 2-faces;
heights $h_i \in [h_{\min}, h_{\max}]$ with typical range
$[0.1, 10]$;
vertex norms $\|v\| \lesssim 100$;
and empirically $\beta_i = O(\|v\|)$, though the precise
scaling of $\beta$ with the polytope geometry (via the
constraints $N^\top \beta = 0$, $\eta^\top \beta = 1$)
deserves further investigation.
Before the normalisation $\eta^\top \beta = 1$,
the un-normalised period~$T$ of the orbit is bounded below
by the inradius (determined by $\min_k h_k$) and above by
the circumradius (determined by $\max_k \|v_k\|$);
the normalisation rescales $\beta$ by $1/T$.

These scales determine how aggressively the three-valued
predicates can distinguish $\textsc{true}$ from
$\textsc{unknown}$:
tolerances that are small relative to these scales yield
few $\textsc{unknown}$ verdicts.
Note that bounds on $h_k$ alone do not give good bounds on
vertex norms~$\|v_k\|$;
the random polytope generator should be checked to confirm
that extreme vertex norms are not generated.
\end{remark}
