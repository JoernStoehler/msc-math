\section{Finding mathematical patterns with data science}
\label{sec:fresh-data-search}
\label{sec:black-box-datascience}

We use data science to look for geometric relationships involving symplectic
capacity. We sample random polytopes and compute a broad range of readily
available geometric quantities, including symplectic invariants and
measurements that can change under symplectic transformations. The sampling
distributions are specified in Section~\ref{subsec:ds-sampling}.

We then look for simple statistical patterns without requiring a geometric
explanation in advance. When a pattern emerges, we return to the definitions
and try to understand why the quantities might be related. This can suggest
a tentative conjecture, which further examples may help sharpen or refute.
The aim is to formulate a precise statement and find a proof that explains
the observed relationship.

The experiments reported below carried out parts of this program. They
found a strong association between symplectic ridge area and the numerical
systolic ratio. Examining the descriptor geometrically clarified what it
measures, but did not establish a general relation to capacity. The pattern
also served a complementary aim: finding bodies with large ratios.
Selection rules based on it improved the results of fresh sampling before
we had an explanation for their success. The chapter follows this signal
from its statistical discovery through geometric interpretation and search
tests, then considers search methods that change the bodies themselves.

\subsection{The sampled bodies and the measured ratio}
\label{subsec:ds-sampling}

For a convex body $K\subset\mathbb R^4$, the target is
\[
 \operatorname{sys}(K)=\frac{c_{\mathrm{EHZ}}(K)^2}{2\operatorname{vol}_4(K)}.
\]
We sought values above one. Multiplying all coordinates by $t>0$ multiplies
capacity by $t^2$ and volume by $t^4$, so a common dilation leaves the ratio
unchanged. The search must change shape or its symplectic alignment.

A polytope was specified by inequalities
\[
 K=\{x:\langle n_i,x\rangle\leq h_i,\quad i=1,\ldots,F\}.
\]
For the generic family, each $n_i$ was independent and uniform on the unit
sphere in $\mathbb R^4$: we normalized a vector of four independent standard
Gaussian coordinates. The heights were independent and uniform on
$[0.8,1.2)$. A proposed body was retained only if it was bounded and every
proposed inequality defined a facet. The construction also rejected zero or
near-duplicate normals. These conditions are part of the sampling law;
``random polytope'' here does not mean a uniform choice from all convex bodies.

The second family consisted of Lagrangian products $P\times Q$. In coordinates
$(q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)$, each factor was a planar polygon constructed in the same
way, using independent uniform angles for its unit normals. A $k\times m$
product has $k$ sides in its first factor and $m$ in its second. We call a
fixed choice of these side counts, or of $F$ in the generic family, a sample
group. The retained exploratory data contained the following groups.

The numerical observation associated with a body is
\[
 s_{\mathrm{num}}=\frac{c_{\mathrm{num}}^2}{2V_{\mathrm{num}}},
\]
where the two inputs are the computed capacity and volume. The largest value
in these 14,336 records was $0.862586$; no stored value exceeded one.
The historical records can include reused cached evaluations, and their
retained source information does not certify every capacity or volume.
Accordingly, this and the historical comparisons below concern recorded
numerical ratios. The newer three pilots use an evaluator with certified
capacity bounds, but still use floating-point volume. Those bounds do not
make the complete ratio certified, or change the status of the older data.

\subsection{A collection of geometric measurements}

The first collection combined face structure with geometry. Face counts,
vertex degrees and incidences record how a polytope is assembled. They can
be computed without capacity and are unchanged by invertible linear maps.
Ridge areas bring the symplectic form into the measurements. We define them
here before asking which quantities were informative; the statistical
analysis was free to find that some contributed very little.


A two-dimensional face of a four-dimensional polytope is called a ridge.
For a ridge $R$ with cyclically ordered vertices $v_0,\ldots,v_{r-1}$, define
its absolute symplectic area by
\[
 A_\omega(R)=\frac12\left|\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\omega_0(v_i,v_{i+1})\right|,
 \qquad v_r=v_0,\qquad
 \omega_0=dq_1\wedge dp_1+dq_2\wedge dp_2.
\]
This is the polygonal area formula with the symplectic pairing in place of
the planar determinant. It measures the area seen by $\omega_0$; a ridge can
have positive Euclidean area and zero symplectic area. Two summaries proved
useful:
\[
 S(K)=\frac{\sum_R A_\omega(R)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{vol}_4(K)}},
 \qquad
 C(K)=\frac{\max_R A_\omega(R)}{\sum_R A_\omega(R)}.
\]
The first records total symplectic ridge area relative to volume. The second
records the fraction concentrated on the largest ridge, when the total is
positive. Both are unchanged by translation, common dilation and linear
symplectic maps. For $S$, the numerator and denominator both scale by $t^2$.
The area formula is translation invariant because the terms involving the
translation vector cancel around the closed polygon.

Later analyses added ordinary Euclidean ridge area $E(R)$ and the ratio
$\kappa(R)=A_\omega(R)/E(R)$ for a ridge of positive area. These separate
its size from the alignment of its plane with the symplectic form, relative
to the Euclidean metric. They need not survive a nonorthogonal symplectic
transformation. Normalizing $E(R)$ by the square root of volume removes
common scaling, but does not give symplectic invariance. Thus the broader
collection contained both invariants and non-invariants. These later
measurements were used to interpret the ridge signal; they were not inputs
to the original 39-feature prediction experiment below.

\subsection{Looking for patterns in the measurements}

We first treated the descriptors as columns of data and asked which ones
ordered or predicted the numerical ratio. Smaller $S$ usually accompanied larger recorded ratios. The Spearman
correlation, which compares the ranks of the two quantities, was $-0.938$.
The ordinary linear correlation was only $-0.205$: the strong relationship
was in the ordering, without being well described by a straight line.
Because the data mix bodies with different facet counts, we also compared
ranks within each of the eighteen sample groups. The association was negative
in every group, with correlations from $-0.997$ to $-0.841$. This reuses the
same data; it checks a possible explanation of the pooled association rather
than supplying an independent repetition.

Prediction experiments gave another view of the signal. A random forest~\cite{dsBreiman2001},
which averages predictions from many decision trees, used 39 descriptors:
27 describing face counts and incidences, and 12 summarizing ridge areas.
The test split held out groups defined by capacity-source label and facet
count, leaving 8,192 training rows and 6,144 test rows. Its test coefficient
of determination was $R^2=0.885$. Here
\[
 R^2=1-\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathrm{test}}(s_i-\widehat s_i)^2}
                   {\sum_{i\in\mathrm{test}}(s_i-\overline s_{\mathrm{test}})^2}
\]
compares squared prediction error with prediction by the test-set mean.
The mean absolute error was $0.0495$. This was one group split, not repeated
cross-validation across independent datasets.

A separate feature-family comparison used histogram-based gradient-boosted
regression~\cite{dsSklearnHGB}, which successively fits trees to improve the prediction. Under
its common training/test split, ridge-area features alone gave $R^2=0.887$,
whereas combinatorial counts alone gave $0.043$. The comparison points to
information in the ridge areas beyond those counts. Its split and feature
list differ from the random-forest experiment, so the two scores do not rank
the learning algorithms against each other.

\subsection{Returning from a pattern to geometry}

The ridge result suggests a first, deliberately imprecise idea: reducing
symplectic area on the two-dimensional faces might favor a large systolic
ratio. This is a starting point for interpretation. It leaves open what
should be held fixed, whether a statement concerns individual bodies or a
sampling distribution, and how face areas could constrain capacity.

The HKO body supplies a useful reference. Its normalized ridge sum is
$8.944272$, close to the low end of the retained sample. However, the sample
minimum is smaller, $8.925918$. Thus even this particularly successful body
does not give a separating rule of the form ``smaller ridge sum than every
ordinary example.'' The observed association motivates selection; it does
not establish a monotone geometric law relating $S$ to capacity.

Products allow us to unpack the descriptor further. Let $u_i$ and $v_j$ be
the oriented edge vectors of $P$ and $Q$. A ridge lying entirely in a $q$-
or $p$-plane has zero symplectic area. A mixed ridge is the rectangle formed
from one edge in each factor, and its symplectic area is $|u_i\cdot v_j|$.
Consequently
\[
 S(P\times Q)=\frac{\sum_{i,j}|u_i\cdot v_j|}
                    {\sqrt{\operatorname{area}(P)\operatorname{area}(Q)}}.
\]
For a fixed $u_i$, the sum over the edges of $Q$ is the total variation of
the projection of its boundary onto the direction of $u_i$. Convexity gives
one increasing and one decreasing chain between the extreme projections,
so
\[
 \sum_j|u_i\cdot v_j|
 =2\|u_i\|\,\operatorname{width}_Q(u_i/\|u_i\|).
\]
The descriptor therefore combines the edge lengths of one factor with the
widths of the other in the edge directions. This gives concrete geometry
to investigate instead of treating the successful column as an unexplained
score.

It also helps test the scope of the tentative idea. For two aligned unit
squares, eight edge pairs have absolute scalar product one and the rest
zero, so $S=8$. Their product is the unit four-dimensional cube, whose
capacity is one and whose systolic ratio is $1/2$.\footnote{For the centered
unit cube the dual rows are $\pm2e_i$. In the variational formula, closure
forces equal weights $x_j$ on the two $q_j$ rows and equal weights $y_j$ on
the two $p_j$ rows, with $\sum_{j=1}^2(x_j+y_j)=1/2$. The order of the four
rows in a coordinate pair gives a contribution at most $8x_jy_j$ to $Q$.
Thus $Q\leq8\sum_jx_jy_j\leq1/2$. A cyclic word on one coordinate pair
with its four weights equal to $1/4$ attains $Q=1/2$, giving $c=1/(2Q)=1$.} HKO has the larger ridge
score $8.944272$ and a ratio greater than one. Thus a universal claim that
a smaller ridge score forces a larger systolic ratio is false. The strong
negative association in the sampled distributions needs a more specific
explanation.

An existing analysis returned to the individual faces through
\[
 A_\omega(R)=E(R)\kappa(R).
\]
In the generic ten-facet selection described below, the low-ridge panel had
smaller mean normalized Euclidean ridge area than controls ($0.917$ versus
$1.365$), and smaller Euclidean-area-weighted alignment factor ($0.377$
versus $0.503$). Both components therefore moved with the coarse filter.
Within the selected panel, however, the ten most extreme ridge scores had
slightly larger ordinary area and smaller alignment factor than the other
ninety, without a larger mean ratio. This decomposition does not isolate a
cause. It explains why the next question cannot simply be which component
to drive lower without considering the rest of the shape.

These observations return us to a sharper mathematical task: identify a
restricted family or controlled change for which the edge-and-width
quantity has a predictable relation to capacity. The current evidence does
not supply that relation. Fresh selection tests establish practical use of
the signal, while the later experiments vary support heights and symplectic alignment.
Those pilots measure changes in ratio; they do not establish which ridge-area
component explains a capacity change.

\subsection{Testing whether a pattern can guide fresh selection}

Alongside geometric interpretation, we tested the signal as a search rule.
A predictor can describe an existing dataset without helping on new bodies. We
therefore changed the order of computation: generate fresh geometry, compute
its descriptors, fix the selected bodies and comparison bodies, and only then
evaluate their ratios. Selection cannot use the target values at this stage.

An initial pool of 100,000 product candidates tested thirty scalar selection
sets. These included low ridge sums and other ridge summaries, as well as
high face counts, selecting globally or separately within side-count groups.
For example, the per-group low-sum rule selected the ten smallest values of
$S$ in each group. The union of all selections and their baselines required
1,675 evaluations, not 100,000. Its maximum recorded ratio was $0.867546$.
Several rules selected the same bodies; their outcomes cannot be added as
independent repetitions. The next experiments isolated more specific rules.

The concentration experiment generated a new pool of 100,000 candidates.
Within each side-count group, it first retained the lowest one percent by
$S$. It then divided this tail into two equally sized parts according to
$C$. The question was whether spreading the already small total symplectic
area more evenly would select better bodies. The half with smaller $C$ had a
mean recorded ratio larger by $0.0340$ than the other half. The difference
was positive in eight of the ten groups. This met the descriptive criterion
fixed before evaluation: a positive aggregate difference and positive
differences in at least seven groups. The run evaluated 2,490 bodies and
reached a maximum of $0.902384$. The criterion was a continuation rule for
this experiment, not a statistical significance test.

A different descriptor came from the primal vertices. Let $\Gamma$ be their
population covariance matrix: subtract the mean vertex and average the outer
products of the centered vectors. Its two Williamson eigenvalues
$0<\nu_1\leq\nu_2$ describe its symplectic scales. Equivalently, the eigenvalues
of $J\Gamma$ are $\pm i\nu_1,\pm i\nu_2$, where
$J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_2\\-I_2&0\end{pmatrix}$.
We used
\[
 \rho(K)=\nu_2/\nu_1
\]
to favor bodies whose vertex covariance has similar scales in the two
symplectic directions. This uses geometry alone. It is unchanged by common
dilation and linear symplectic transformations, as well as translation
because the covariance is centered.

Two independently seeded pools of 50,000 products compared three selections
in every side-count group: the lowest $0.5\%$ by $\rho$, the ridge rule above
(lowest $1\%$ by $S$, then its lower half by $C$), and 25 disjoint controls.
There were 500 memberships in each selection across the two pools. Because
64 bodies belonged to both geometric selections, 1,436 distinct targets
were evaluated.

The mean difference between the covariance selection and controls was
$0.333$, with an interval $[0.310,0.356]$. Against the ridge selection, the
difference was $0.0145$, with interval $[-0.0149,0.0439]$. These intervals
use the twenty seed-by-group differences and a Student-$t$ calculation with
nineteen degrees of freedom. They do not represent twenty independent seeds.
The covariance rule selected much larger recorded ratios than controls,
while this comparison did not establish an advantage over ridge selection.
No recorded value above one was found in these selection experiments.

\subsection{Transfer and the limit of a stronger filter}

The ridge observation first came from a mixture of generic polytopes and
products. To test a concrete selection rule on generic ten-facet bodies, we
generated 10,000 fresh candidates and selected the lowest one percent by
\emph{mean} normalized ridge area, $S(K)$ divided by the ridge count.
Generic bodies with the same facet count can have different ridge counts,
so this differs from selecting by the sum. The 100 selected bodies had mean
recorded ratio $0.626$, compared with $0.339$ for 100 disjoint controls.
The difference was $0.287$, with a percentile-bootstrap interval
$[0.250,0.324]$ from 20,000 resamples of the realized panels.

The same experiment asked whether a more extreme filter would help further.
The ten bodies in the lowest $0.1\%$ were compared with the other ninety
selected bodies. Their mean difference was $-0.0318$, with interval
$[-0.0946,0.0331]$. This failed the criterion for expanding that particular
search to a larger pool. It did not establish that stronger filtering is
harmful: the interval permits effects of either sign. The useful contrast
is that a coarse geometric filter worked well while the next restriction
did not show an additional gain.

We also changed the product sampling law. Starting from the original random
normals and heights, we replaced every support height in both factors by
one. Each side of the resulting polygon lies on a tangent line to the unit
circle. We required both the original factors and the changed factors to
be admissible, and then separately normalized the two changed factors to
area one. This produces a conditioned family of tangential polygons.

On one fresh pool with 3,200 eligible bodies in each of the $4\times6$ and
$6\times6$ groups, ridge and covariance selection were compared with shared,
disjoint controls. Each selection had sixteen memberships per group; five
selected bodies overlapped, giving 91 actual evaluations. Averaging the two
group effects equally, relative to controls, covariance selection gained $0.259$
and ridge selection gained $0.225$. Their respective resampling intervals were
$[0.192,0.329]$ and $[0.160,0.296]$. These intervals condition on the realized
panels; one seed and two side-count groups do not measure variation across
arbitrary source laws. The shared controls also make the comparisons
dependent. The experiment nevertheless gives a concrete instance in which
both rules remained useful after changing how bodies were generated.

\subsection{Changing support heights and symplectic alignment}

The transfer experiment selected among tangential products. It did not
isolate the effect of making a particular polygon tangential. For that
question we kept the normals fixed and compared the original product with
three changes: equalize the first factor's heights, equalize the second
factor's heights, or equalize both. Sixteen independently generated pairs,
eight of type $4\times4$ and eight of type $4\times6$, supplied 64 bodies.
We accepted a pair only if all four constructions were valid, before
evaluating any ratio. The paired result therefore concerns this jointly
admissible source.

Each factor was then scaled to area one. Separate factor dilations preserve
the mathematical ratio, since
\[
 \operatorname{diag}(aI_2,bI_2)
 =\sqrt{ab}\,\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{a/b}\,I_2,\sqrt{b/a}\,I_2)
\]
is a common dilation followed by a symplectic map. Replacing unequal support
heights is the shape change being tested. Changing both factors raised the
mean numerical ratio by $0.0153$: eleven pairs improved and five declined.
The mean changes were $0.0040$ for $4\times4$ and $0.0266$ for $4\times6$.
All 64 evaluations succeeded. An average improvement therefore did not give
a rule that equalizing the heights helps every body.

Rotation changes a different aspect of the geometry. An orthogonal map
preserves Euclidean shape but need not preserve $\omega_0$. A rotated
Lagrangian product can consequently have a different capacity and need no
longer be a Lagrangian product for the fixed symplectic form.

We compared two allocations of sixteen evaluations. One evaluated a fresh
product, a symplectic rotation as a control, and fourteen prescribed
orthogonal rotations. The other evaluated sixteen fresh products, sharing
the first body with the rotation allocation. We repeated the comparison
four times, twice for $3\times3$ and twice for $4\times4$. The rotation
schedule and all source bodies were fixed before target evaluation. Shared
starts gave 124 distinct requests and 128 charged memberships. The
symplectic controls preserved the numerical ratios within $4\cdot10^{-16}$.

Rotation improved three of the four starting bodies. Fresh sampling still
produced the larger maximum in every comparison. In one $4\times4$ case,
rotation raised the ratio from $0.328$ to $0.529$, while fresh sampling
reached $0.774$. The rotation evaluations also took longer. For these four
starts and this prescribed schedule, the same number of evaluations was
more effective when spent on fresh bodies.

\subsection{Adapting the sampling distribution}

Fresh sampling won the rotation comparison, but it repeatedly draws from the
same law. We next tested whether the evaluated bodies could improve that law.
The cross-entropy method~\cite{dsDeBoer2005} used here repeatedly fits a simple distribution to
the best part of a population, then samples another population from it.

This experiment used $5\times5$ products. We described each pentagon by the
cyclic gaps between its five normal angles and the relative lengths of its
five dual normals. There are four independent angular-gap coordinates and
four independent logarithmic lengths per factor. One relative angle between
the factors gives seventeen coordinates altogether. Separate factor scales
and a simultaneous planar rotation do not change the mathematical ratio,
which allows their removal from this description. The precise coordinates
and update are given below so that adapting the distribution has a concrete
meaning.

Both arms began with the same 64 independent products from the original
angle-and-height source. The adaptive arm fitted a diagonal Gaussian in these
coordinates, then moved its mean and coordinate variances halfway toward
those of the sixteen bodies with largest numerical ratio. It drew another
64 bodies, evaluated them, and repeated this update. Each arm had four
populations including initialization, for 256 charged evaluations. The
comparison arm continued independent sampling throughout. A variance floor
kept the adaptive sampler from eliminating a coordinate's variation entirely.

Three seeds produced the following results. The top-eight statistic is the
median of the eight highest ratios after removing repeated, exactly equal
dual-vertex arrays in an arm; different bodies with equal scores remain. It tests
whether improvement extends beyond a single best observation.

The source restriction needs particular care. An initial version stopped
when one body exceeded the evaluator's admitted primal-vertex infinity-norm range.
The completed version applied the evaluator's existing admission conditions
to both proposal arms before accepting a body for evaluation. This was a
procedural revision after seeing the failure. Both arms reject unsupported
inputs, but the admission test acts on the actual coordinate representation:
IID bodies retain their original representation and adaptive bodies use the
normalized reconstruction. It is not a symmetry-invariant condition on all
pentagon products. The result is therefore for this implemented,
admission-conditioned comparison.

The complete run took $215.8$ seconds: $182.2$ seconds for construction and
admission checks, $32.9$ seconds in evaluator processes, and $0.053$ seconds
for distribution updates. Including construction and charging initialization
to each arm, the adaptive runs used about $35$--$36$ seconds per seed and IID
about $46$--$51$ seconds. Equal evaluation counts did not imply equal cost.
These measured costs favor the adaptive arm in this run; they are not the
outcome of a separate equal-wall-time experiment.

\subsection{Local search from matched starting bodies}
\label{subsec:black-box-datascience-finite-budget-optimization}

The adaptive sampler changes a distribution over products. A separate,
earlier experiment changed the dual normals of generic ten-facet bodies
directly. Seven fixed implementations started from the same 64 bodies.
Each run allowed at most 128 charged full evaluations and a one-second
accumulated evaluator-plus-optimizer time ceiling, excluding the initial
evaluation. Invalid proposals still consumed calls. Work begun below the
time ceiling was allowed to finish, so terminal results can include a small
overrun; they are not values available at exactly one second.

The capacity calculation suggests local branch functions, each associated
with a particular candidate in the variational problem. When several smooth
functions compete through their minimum, following the gradient of one
selected function can fail to increase the minimum. For example, the minimum
of $x$ and $-x$ decreases in both directions away from zero, although either
individual function has a direction of increase. This motivates methods
that include more than the currently selected branch.

The literal gradient implementation took a fixed step along that branch's
gradient and accepted usable evaluations even when the ratio decreased.
The safeguarded version removed the symmetry directions, normalized the
gradient, and accepted only improvements; its relative step length expanded
by $1.25$ after success and contracted by $0.5$ after failure. A finite-gap
model included branches whose actions were within ten percent of the
minimum and maximized the minimum of their affine predictions in a small
ball. Its proposal still required a full evaluation and a strict improvement.
The finite branch list is a local model, not a complete formula for all
branches near the body.

Two history methods retained candidates from the current and up to three
previous accepted anchors, then used this larger list for two inner model
steps before full evaluation. One also predicted additional candidates from
branch transitions for sufficiently long steps. Their outer rule accepted
any usable evaluation, so a move could reduce the objective even while the
best value seen so far stayed unchanged. The remaining methods were a
Gaussian population search with covariance adaptation (CMA-ES)~\cite{dsHansen2016}, and a poll
of both signs of a transverse coordinate basis, keeping the best improvement.

Most methods used the complement of the fifteen translation, scaling and
linear symplectic tangent directions. This leaves twenty-five directions
at a generic ten-facet body. The coordinate poll and local model methods
rebuilt their transverse space at their anchors; CMA-ES used its initial
space, and the literal gradient used the ambient coordinates. These are
choices in the tested implementations, not interchangeable realizations of
one global quotient chart.

\subsection{What a failed local probe tells us}

A large terminal value also raises a geometric question: has search arrived
at a local maximum? Evaluating a finite set of nearby points can discover an
improvement, but failing to find one does not prove local maximality.
The endpoint diagnostics gave concrete examples of this distinction.

Sixteen endpoints of the history method were chosen across the observed
performance range of the common starts. At each, we evaluated both signs of
twenty-five transverse basis directions at relative radii $10^{-3}$,
$10^{-4}$ and $10^{-5}$: 150 probes per endpoint. Seven endpoints had at least
one raw positive change and nine had none. The sixteen endpoints were
selected by outcome ranks, so these fractions do not estimate a population
convergence probability.

After allowing a larger search budget on those starts, a further continuation
experiment examined three selected optimizer endpoints and HKO. It tested
branch-model and selected-gradient proposals at five decreasing step lengths,
falling back to a signed basis poll, and allowed at most ten accepted
improvements. All three optimizer endpoints took ten improving steps. One
had previously decreased along every tested signed basis direction, yet
improved by $0.00223$ along a sequence of oblique moves with total normalized
path length $0.01$. HKO accepted no move. The theorem establishes HKO's local
status; the finite search supplies a control observation consistent with it.

Another fixed suite probed a regular triangle--hexagon product, a rotated
square--square product and the CH2021 example, together with a regular
pentagon product chosen on a known increasing rotation path. It combined
signed transverse basis directions, projected random directions and
prescribed rotations at three radii. All 1,482 probes were valid. None of
the three target bodies had a nominal gain above $10^{-12}$, while the
pentagon control had twelve, all in the rotation probes. Its generic basis
and random probes found none. Even this control therefore shows that a
finite collection of generic directions can miss an available increase.
The target misses remain numerical diagnostics; they do not exclude
untested directions or changes in facet count. The historical run also has
incomplete source provenance, and its broad capacity-derived bounds do not
turn the nominal target misses into a proof.

\subsection{What the experiments contribute}

The exploratory approach found a strong ridge-area pattern, then gave it
more concrete meaning through edge widths and the area--alignment
decomposition. That interpretation ruled out a universal monotonicity claim
and left a more restricted capacity relation to be formulated. The search
approach found geometric information useful before capacity evaluation. Low ridge area and balanced vertex covariance selected larger
recorded ratios in fresh pools, and some of that usefulness survived changes
in the generating law. More severe ridge filtering did not automatically
improve selection. Changing individual bodies also had varied effects:
equalizing heights helped on average but harmed some pairs, while prescribed
rotations lost to fresh sampling under the tested call allocation. Adapting
the product distribution improved on IID in three small paired runs.

The local-search comparison obtained larger recorded values from models
that retained several candidate branches. Its endpoint checks also exposed
improving directions missed by a basis poll. These are distinct experimental
results, with different sources and evaluation contracts. Together they
suggest useful ways to continue searching, while leaving the central
challenge of reaching new high-ratio bodies unresolved. The initial sample, prospective selections, three new pilots and matched-start
optimizer runs produced no stored value above one. This observation excludes
the deliberately supplied HKO and pentagon controls; it neither proves

\subsection*{Computational details for the adaptive product sampler}

For reproducibility, let $g_1,\ldots,g_5>0$ be the cyclic normal-angle gaps
of a factor and let $r_i$ be its dual-normal lengths. Set
\[
 \ell_i=\log(g_i/g_5)\quad(i=1,\ldots,4),\qquad
 z_i=\log r_i-\frac15\sum_{j=1}^5\log r_j.
\]
We store the first four $z_i$ and recover $z_5=-\sum_{i=1}^4z_i$.
Reconstruction uses
\[
 g_i=\frac{2\pi e^{\ell_i}}{1+\sum_{j=1}^4e^{\ell_j}},\qquad
 g_5=\frac{2\pi}{1+\sum_{j=1}^4e^{\ell_j}},\qquad h_i=e^{-z_i}.
\]
The first normal angle is zero in the first factor and $\phi$ in the second.
The initial IID encoding chooses a cyclic origin by lexicographically
minimizing the sequence of gap/length pairs. Sampled adaptive coordinates
are retained for the updates without another cyclic relabelling.

For an ordinary coordinate $j$, write $\widehat\mu_{E,j}$ and
$\widehat v_{E,j}$ for the mean and population variance of the sixteen elites.
The update is
\[
 \mu_j^+=\tfrac12\mu_j+\tfrac12\widehat\mu_{E,j},\qquad
 v_j^+=\max\{\tfrac12v_j+\tfrac12\widehat v_{E,j},\;0.05v_{0,j}\}.
\]
Initialization uses the population moments of the shared 64 bodies.
Coordinates are drawn independently with these Gaussian moments. The phase
uses circular means, a unit-vector blend of old and elite means, and variance
of angular deviations wrapped into $[-\pi,\pi)$. Samples are wrapped modulo
$2\pi$. A vanishing resultant retains the previous mean; initial fallback
uses the smallest-ID candidate's phase. Ties in elite selection are broken
by candidate ID.

Each population permits at most 640 construction attempts for 64 accepted
bodies. Failed geometry and unsupported evaluator inputs are rejected before
target evaluation; an incomplete population does not trigger an update.
The evaluator admission includes finite, nonredundant bounded geometry and
primal and dual infinity-norm bounds between $10^{-3}$ and $10^3$.
