Research night · 4–5 October 2026 · Account for Jörn, revised 5 October

We strengthened HKO's case.
We did not finish the research.

The night produced a reviewed local theorem and a new global theorem for a restricted family of pentagon products. The attempts to reach broader global statements stopped at identifiable mathematical gaps.

The strongest completed results concern HKO, the product of two regular pentagons. A second body, P*, gained some protection against new-facet perturbations, but its corresponding eleven-facet theorem is still incomplete. The manuscript and its retained PDF were not changed.

What were we trying to find out?

Can a convex body beat HKO's ratio of symplectic capacity to volume? There are two very different ways to attack that question: deform HKO slightly, or compare it with distant shapes. Success in the first problem does not settle the second.

sys(C) = cEHZ(C)² / (2 vol4(C))

HKO has sys = (3 + √5)/5 ≈ 1.04721. P* has ratio 1. So proving another body locally maximal at 1 would reveal another local maximum, without making it a competitor at HKO's higher value.

How the night unfolded

The work followed three scientific strands, with independent checks of their returns. This is the order of the main mathematical developments, not a minute-by-minute activity log.

First: test the existing HKO proof

A returned proof packet already claimed local maximality with up to eleven facets. The night checked its finite certificates in a fresh copy and separately reviewed the endpoint and limiting-sequence arguments. Those checks supported accepting the stated theorem.

Then: enlarge the global pentagon family

With one regular pentagon fixed, the other factor was first allowed arbitrary side offsets and rotation, then a fixed-axis distortion as well. Complete parameter and boundary checks supported the sharp HKO bound throughout that family, with only regular HKO equality.

Meanwhile: try new-facet perturbations of P*

The work established strict loss for shallow cuts in fixed directions, and controlled some open direction patches even while the old facets move. It did not assemble all directions into one full neighborhood theorem.

Finally: expose the next global obstacle

Allowing a general invertible matrix on the partner's normal directions led to an exact seven-variable cone with 610 linear constraints. The remaining quadratic inequality was not proved. The broader continuation was parked, and execution ended at the requested 04:55 Berlin release, before the 05:00 cutoff.

The local theorem was reviewed, a restricted global theorem was extended, and the broad problems remained open. That is why completed assignments did not amount to “research finished.”

NEW GLOBAL RESULT · RESTRICTED FAMILY

Which pentagons are now covered?

Keep the regular pentagon Q fixed. Build the partner K by choosing five normal directions, rotating them, stretching their horizontal components, and moving their supporting lines. The theorem covers every valid choice in that family, including limits where a side disappears.

Regular HKO: the equality case in this family.

The drawing computes intersections of the displayed half-planes. It does not compute capacity or certify the theorem. Only one of the five offsets is exposed here; the theorem allows all five.

Throughout this family, sys(K ×L Q) ≤ 1.04721…. Equality occurs exactly at a regular partner, with the HKO relative angles, up to translation and positive dilation.

The axes of distortion stay fixed relative to Q. A general transformation of the normal directions has another freedom that these controls do not include. The theorem also does not cover arbitrary five-direction fans or arbitrary pentagon pairs.

Why moving the sides cannot beat HKO in the regular-direction case

For regular normal directions, the proof identifies capacity with the size of the largest translated regular pentagon inside K, times a factor depending on the relative angle.

Once that inner size is fixed, extra area around it only increases the denominator of the ratio. The regular inner pentagon is therefore the best partner at that angle. Optimizing the angle gives HKO.

This explains the regular-direction theorem. Stretching the normal directions breaks that simple capacity identity; the larger family needs the separate complete-minimizer argument.

Why this is more than a grid of successful examples

The proof reduces the area minimum at capacity one to a complete finite list of candidate support configurations. Exact sign arguments then cover the continuous rotation/stretch parameters, their boundary ties, and unbounded tails. Independent review also checks the physical area/capacity normalization and equality cases. The passage from every support vector to that finite list is a mathematical argument; finite arithmetic alone would not supply it.

REVIEWED LOCAL RESULT · HKO

What does “up to eleven facets” add?

HKO starts with ten facets. Moving those ten bounding hyperplanes tests the existing shape. A small truncation can create an eleventh facet, introducing a kind of perturbation the ten-facet theorem did not cover.

Accepted statement: some Hausdorff neighborhood of HKO contains no convex four-polytope with at most eleven genuine facets and a larger ratio.

Old normals may move. The new facet may appear at a scale different from those motions. The review covered the finite predicates and the arguments needed to handle these coupled limits.

The result gives no explicit neighborhood radius. Twelve or more facets and arbitrary smooth perturbations remain outside it.

For P*, the result is narrower. Some direction patches resist new facets even with old-facet motion, and every fixed codimension-at-least-two cut direction loses ratio when the cut is sufficiently shallow. But “each fixed direction eventually works” does not establish one uniform neighborhood covering every varying direction. That assembly is still missing.

Other things we learned—and what they change

Balance does not certify a maximum

A regular decagon partner satisfies the relevant coverage and balance checks, yet an exact deformation keeps its capacity and reduces area. Its ratio therefore improves. This gives a concrete warning against using those checks as a sufficient maximality test.

A scalar shortcut failed

An exact quadrilateral violated a proposed inequality intended to simplify the arbitrary-partner problem. This eliminated that proof route; it did not refute the HKO bound.

Bulk measurements do not determine capacity

Two bodies have the same covariance and volume but different capacities. Those summaries alone cannot determine capacity. This leaves possible one-sided bounds and useful statistical predictors as separate questions.

One nonlinear equality construction is blocked

For the specified convex cotangent images of products, the base map must be affine under the stated normal-direction hypothesis. That restricts this construction method; it does not classify all equality bodies.
