From a ridge-area signal to a tested selection rule

Cheap geometric measurements could help search for large systolic ratios if they identify promising bodies before capacity is evaluated. The initial evidence is a strong association, but three different questions must be distinguished: whether a measurement orders a sampled population, whether geometry forces that ordering in a particular family, and whether a frozen rule enriches a newly generated population.

To test practical selection, candidates must be chosen without their targets. In the initial fresh pool of 100,000 products, selecting the ten smallest ridge sums per side-count group gave mean numerical ratio 0.6260.626, against 0.3160.316 for equal-size, disjoint hash-selected controls. Thirty scalar selections and their controls together required 1,675 distinct capacity evaluations, not 100,000. Further restriction was less reliable: in another low-ridge panel, groupwise rank correlations ranged from −0.315-0.315 to +0.301+0.301. A separate test nevertheless found incremental information in ridge concentration: among the lowest one percent by RR, the half with smaller largest-ridge share (maximum area divided by total area) had a mean advantage of 0.0340.034. These comparisons establish finite enrichment without a universal ordering inside the selected tail.

A changed-source test asks whether that usefulness survives one specified change of distribution. The factorial-both source sets support heights to one, requires both original and changed factors to be admissible, and separately normalizes changed factors to area one. One seed supplied 3,200 eligible products in each of the 4×64\times6 and 6×66\times6 groups. Two previously frozen rules selected sixteen per group: the lowest 0.5%0.5\% by vertex-covariance imbalance ρ=ν2/ν1\rho=\nu_2/\nu_1, where the ordered νi\nu_i are the Williamson eigenvalues of the centered covariance of distinct vertices, and the lowest 1%1\% by RR followed by its lower half in largest-ridge share. Each group also supplied sixteen disjoint controls. Five overlaps between selectors left 91 distinct evaluations.

Both rules raised mean numerical ratio in both groups. Equally weighted gains were 0.2590.259 for covariance and 0.2250.225 for ridge selection. None exceeded one. Shared controls and overlapping selections make the comparisons dependent; resampling ranges condition on these realized panels. The result is a successful transfer to this particular conditioned source, with no established mechanism, covariance superiority, or stability across other seeds and laws. Together, the exact family and the generated tests support geometric interpretation and useful finite selection while keeping their different evidential strengths visible.
