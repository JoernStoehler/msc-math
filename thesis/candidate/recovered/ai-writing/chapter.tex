\section{AI in the research process}
\label{sec:use-of-ai}

The use of AI in this project changed as the available models became more
capable. My experience was that they supplied increasingly useful research
ideas and could carry out longer tasks with fewer interruptions. This changed
what I could delegate. A request could describe a mathematical or experimental
outcome and leave the agent to choose intermediate steps, write code, inspect
the results and revise its approach. The separate disclosure records AI use;
here I discuss what this experience suggests about organizing mathematical
work with agents.

\subsection{Delegating an incomplete proof strategy}

The local maximality proof is the clearest example of how much of a research
task could be delegated. I asked a GPT-5-series model for a proof that the
HKO body is a local maximum. I suggested looking for several upper bounds
and somehow showing that together they control a neighborhood after
quotienting out the symmetries. This was only the overall shape of a
strategy. I had not specified a finite certificate or worked out how the
upper bounds should be constructed.

The model developed essentially the entire argument from that starting
point. It identified a sufficient configuration of twenty-six upper bounds
in the twenty-five transverse dimensions. An initial derivative-based
approach had provided only twenty-five nonsingular cases. The model then
used the weights $\beta$ in the quadratic objective to construct feasible
sections: an invertible $5\times5$ closure minor lets five weights depend
smoothly on the facet parameters while the remaining weights stay fixed.
This supplied upper bounds even where the initial approach failed. It also
completed proof steps involving the volume and wrote the SageMath code for
the exact computations.

The assignment was therefore a conjecture and an incomplete proof idea;
the result was a developed proof together with computational verification
code. The derivative conditions and the form of the finite certificate
were part of the model's contribution, not conditions I supplied in the
request. The useful specification was the mathematical outcome---a proof
of local maximality---while the model had substantial freedom to discover
what would establish it.

\subsection{Matching the check to the claim}

The different parts of this thesis require different evidence. In the HKO
argument, arithmetic checks establish a finite statement about explicitly
defined matrices and weights. The written proof still has to identify those
matrices with derivatives of upper bounds for the geometric quantity. It
also has to pass from labelled normals to a neighborhood of convex bodies.
Omitting either connection would leave a mathematical gap even if every
computed identity were correct.

For the search experiments, a high observed value answers a different
question from a proof of a local maximum. The comparison of prescribed
rotations with fresh sampling makes the distinction concrete: rotations
improved several starting bodies, but fresh sampling reached the larger
maximum in each of the four comparisons. An instruction merely to find an
improvement could have returned a successful example while leaving the
allocation question unanswered. Here the control determines what we learn
from the computation.

The same issue occurs in more routine work. A retained code-repair example
included a regression test that passed even when the original sign defect
was restored. Testing the test against that defect revealed what an ordinary
successful run did not establish. Such checks are useful because they target
a particular possible mistake, rather than adding another undifferentiated
approval of the result.

\subsection{What still requires a reader}

Mathematical exposition introduces a further difficulty. An agent can recover
the meaning of a dense paragraph and judge it correct while a human reader
has to stop repeatedly to decipher it. The intended reader of this thesis
knows smooth symplectic geometry, but still needs an introduction to the
nonsmooth variational problem and its finite reduction. A later definition
does not remove the effort spent reading an earlier sentence that needed it.

This makes reading feedback valuable even when individual calculations have
been checked. The useful feedback identifies where an explanation ceases to
work: a missing implication, an unfamiliar object used before its definition,
or a paragraph whose contribution remains unclear. Whether an agent can
predict that feedback is itself an empirical question. Its ability to
summarize a chapter does not establish that the chapter is comfortable to
read. Nor does an automated check decide whether a mathematically correct
side result deserves the space it occupies. These judgments concern the
reader and the purpose of the thesis.

\subsection{A connection with training on verifiable outcomes}

There is a suggestive connection to reinforcement learning with verifiable
rewards. Guo and collaborators trained DeepSeek-R1-Zero using rule-based
accuracy and format rewards; mathematical answers and code test outcomes
supplied checkable feedback. Their later DeepSeek-R1 combined reinforcement
learning with supervised fine-tuning and also used model-based rewards for
general tasks~\cite{aiGuo2025}. This is evidence that outcome feedback can
shape learned problem-solving behavior, not that every useful judgment has
a reliable automatic reward.

An outcome supplied in an agent's task context is not a training reward:
the task does not thereby update the model's parameters. The practical
connection is that both require a criterion aligned with the intended
achievement. In the HKO example, the finite conditions that could be checked
were themselves discovered during the research task. They were not a test
suite supplied in advance. My experience motivates using agents for work
with clear outcomes and checking whether the returned evidence actually
supports them. It does not isolate the contribution of a particular training
method or measure the time this project would have taken without AI.
