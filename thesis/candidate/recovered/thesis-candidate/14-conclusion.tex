\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The HKO body is a local maximum of the systolic ratio among ten-facet
polytopes. Every sufficiently nearby body in this space has ratio at most
$(3+\sqrt5)/5$, and equality occurs precisely under translations, positive
dilations and linear symplectic maps. This answers the local-improvement
question within the fixed-facet space. Deformations that introduce new
facets require a different local model.

A global result gives the same sharp bound for products of independently
linearly deformed regular pentagons. Its equality cases are positive dilates
of linear symplectic images of HKO. Together, the two theorems rule out
improvement by sufficiently small ten-facet perturbations and by arbitrary
deformations within this family of affinely regular products. The latter
can be large, while the former can destroy the product structure.

The placement of the factor planes matters even when the Euclidean product
shape is fixed. Every symplectic product of full-dimensional planar convex
bodies admits a capacity-increasing rigid rotation. The Lagrangian endpoint
can nevertheless have smaller capacity; in the triangle example the maximum
along the path lies strictly between the endpoints. Thus the familiar
symplectic and Lagrangian arrangements need not contain the best position
of a given product.

The local proof also gives a useful way to work at a singular optimization
problem. One can continue feasible choices and use their values as upper
bounds, without continuing stationary optimizers. At HKO, finitely many
such bounds suffice: their derivatives force a strict decrease in every
nonzero direction transverse to the symmetries. The algebraic
calculation establishes the finite conditions, while the geometric proof
identifies the upper bounds and relates their coordinates to nearby bodies.

The finite methods also support computation beyond these proofs. The
Haim--Kislev formula gives a finite capacity problem for convex polytopes,
and the product reduction shows that some maximizing word uses at most three
facet directions from each planar factor. Its implementation supplies the
capacity evaluations used to investigate broader populations. Our flow-graph
construction uses the local passage geometry of Chaidez and Hutchings and
provides a second capacity algorithm under explicit regularity assumptions.

In those populations, symplectic ridge area gives a strong coarse predictive
signal for computed systolic ratios. Geometric selection enriched fresh
samples. The pooled inverse rank association weakens substantially when the
original data are restricted within each sampling group to low ridge scores
or high computed ratios, with ranks recomputed in the retained groups.
Thus a strong ordering across the broad population becomes much weaker
among selected candidates. The distinction between prediction and ascent
is also visible in exact examples: the cube has both a lower ridge score and a lower systolic ratio
than HKO. A universal inverse ordering is therefore false. At the same
time, equation~\ref{eq:ds-pentagon-ridge-capacity} proves an inverse-square
law throughout the regular-pentagon rotation family, so the descriptor does
capture a precise relation on a restricted family.

The broader association still needs an explanation. One possibility is that
typical geometric perturbations in the sampled populations move the two
quantities in opposite directions. Another is that ridge area is a noisy
proxy for a different geometric relation. The experiments do not separate
these hypotheses; restricting the range can itself attenuate correlation.
The search comparisons also show that selection,
deformation and adaptation answer different questions: improving a starting
body need not outperform spending the same evaluations on fresh samples.
None of the exploratory searches produced a new numerical ratio above one.

Two mathematical questions follow directly. Does HKO remain locally maximal
when the facet count changes? Which geometric restrictions explain the ridge
association, and do they survive a change of sampling distribution? The
first asks how far the proved local extremality extends. The second asks
how a useful statistical signal can become a statement about capacity.
