2     Preliminaries
The later computations replace a boundary-dynamics problem by finite data from the facets of
a polytope. This section fixes the common language needed for that passage: action and contact
normalization, the EHZ capacity and systolic ratio, normalized facet data and Lagrangian
products, and finally Clarke’s position-free dual problem. The next section will specialize these
objects to polytopes and prove that the capacity problem contains a finite pure-facet minimizer.
    All convex bodies below are compact convex subsets of R4 with non-empty interior. Unless
stated otherwise, the origin lies in the interior.

2.3   EHZ capacity
For smooth convex bodies, the Ekeland–Hofer and Hofer–Zehnder capacities coincide. The
common value is the least action needed to close up a characteristic trajectory on the boundary.
We use the resulting EHZ capacity in the form

                  cEHZ (K) = min{A(γ) : γ is a closed characteristic on ∂K}.                   (4)

This is the smooth least-action characterization of [AO14, Theorem 2.2]. Equivalently, the mini-
mum may be taken over Reeb orbits. The contact normalization only chooses the parametrization
of each characteristic, while the action is a line integral and is invariant under orientation-
preserving reparametrization. For a contact-normalized representative, A(γ) is also the period.
The same least-action theorem gives a positive attained minimum. For nonsmooth convex
bodies, and in particular for polytopes, the same capacity is obtained by approximation and by


                                                  9
minimizing over the generalized characteristics of Definition 2.1 [AO14, Proposition 2.7]. Their
finite facet description on a polytope is derived in Section 3.
    As a symplectic capacity on convex bodies, cEHZ is monotone under inclusion, invariant
under symplectomorphisms, and conformal:

                                 cEHZ (λK) = λ2 cEHZ (K)      (λ > 0).

It is normalized by
                                 cEHZ (B 4 (r)) = cEHZ (Z 4 (r)) = πr2 ,
where B 4 (r) is the radius-r ball and Z 4 (r) = B 2 (r) × R2 is the symplectic cylinder. These
axioms are used only as background; the finite computations below use the minimum-action
and quadratic-program descriptions.

Definition 2.2 (Systolic-ratio symmetries). Let

                      Gsys = {x 7→ b + ρM x : b ∈ R4 , ρ > 0, M ∈ Sp(4, R)}.

This is the group of affine transformations generated by translations, positive scalings, and linear
symplectic transformations.

Lemma 2.3 (Systolic-ratio invariance). For every convex body K ⊂ R4 and every g ∈ Gsys ,

                                          sys(gK) = sys(K).

Proof. Translations and linear symplectic transformations are symplectomorphisms, so they
preserve cEHZ . They also preserve four-dimensional volume, because translations do not change
volume and every symplectic matrix has determinant 1. A positive scaling by ρ gives

                       cEHZ (ρK) = ρ2 cEHZ (K),         vol4 (ρK) = ρ4 vol4 (K).

Substituting these identities in (5) shows that the ratio is unchanged.

    This is a geometric symmetry group acting on unlabelled convex bodies. In a labelled
coordinate chart, the same unlabelled body may also be represented after relabelling facets or
inequalities; that relabelling ambiguity is a coordinate issue, not an additional element of Gsys .

Proposition 2.4 (Continuity). Let Kt be a family of four-dimensional convex bodies which
is continuous in the Hausdorff topology. Then cEHZ (Kt ) is continuous in t. If, moreover,
t 7→ vol4 (Kt ) is positive, finite, and continuous, then the systolic ratio of the family is continuous.

Proof. The EHZ capacity is C 0 -continuous on convex bodies with respect to the Hausdorff metric
[CH21]. The assertion for sys is then ordinary continuity of the quotient in (5). In the applications
below the volume hypothesis is usually automatic, since four-volume is continuous under
Hausdorff convergence of convex bodies; for the rotated-polygon family it is even constant.

                 Kq ×L Kp = {(q1 , q2 , p1 , p2 ) : (q1 , q2 ) ∈ Kq , (p1 , p2 ) ∈ Kp }.   (7)

2.6   Clarke’s dual action principle
A curve constrained to lie on ∂K is difficult to modify: changing one velocity can move the
rest of it off the boundary. We will instead work with closed curves in R4 and a functional
that depends only on velocity. The dual action principle says that minimizing this functional
recovers the capacity, and that a minimizer can be placed back on the boundary. This is the
freedom used in the next section to rearrange facet velocities.
