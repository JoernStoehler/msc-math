\subsection{Systolic Ratios of 4D Polytopes}
\label{sec:systolic-ratios}

Viterbo's conjecture asserts that the systolic ratio
\[
  \mathrm{sys}(K) \;=\; \frac{c_{\mathrm{EHZ}}(K)^2}{2 \operatorname{vol}(K)}
  \;\le\; 1
\]
holds for all convex bodies~$K \subset \mathbb{R}^{2n}$. Haim-Kislev~(2024)
gave a 10-facet 4-polytope with~$\mathrm{sys}(K) > 1$, disproving the
conjecture. We explore how common such counterexamples are in the space of
random 4-polytopes.

\subsubsection*{Computational setup}

We compute systolic ratios using:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{EHZ capacity}:~$c_{\mathrm{EHZ}}(K)$ is the minimum action
    over all generalized Reeb orbits. By the Haim-Kislev 2017 theorem, it
    suffices to search piecewise linear orbits using pure facet Reeb vectors.
    Implementation in the \texttt{hk2017} Rust crate.
  \item \textbf{Volume}: computed via vertex enumeration and triangulation.
  \item \textbf{Dataset}: random 4-polytopes generated by rejection sampling
    (see Section~\ref{sec:rejection-sampling}), with facet
    counts~$F \in \{5, 6, \ldots, 14\}$ and height
    range~$h \in [0.5, 2.0]$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Pentagon verification.} The pentagon counterexample achieves
$\mathrm{sys}(K) = 1.047 > 1$, confirming the violation of Viterbo's
conjecture. After the optimizations in Section~\ref{sec:optimization}, this
computation takes~504\,ms (10 facets).

\subsubsection*{Random polytope exploration}

We computed systolic ratios for 337 random 4-polytopes across facet
counts~$F = 5, \ldots, 14$, with 50 samples each at $F = 5$--$10$, decreasing
to 20 at~$F = 11$, 10 at~$F = 12$, 5 at~$F = 13$, and 2 at~$F = 14$.
Heights sampled in~$[0.5, 2.0]$ via rejection sampling. Key statistics:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Systolic ratio range}: $[0.0002, 0.718]$ with mean~0.240,
    median~0.209
  \item \textbf{Viterbo violations}: \textbf{0 out of 337} random polytopes
    have $\mathrm{sys}(K) > 1$
  \item \textbf{Capacity range}: $[2.47, 78.0]$ with mean~11.3
  \item \textbf{Volume range}: $[11.1, 781\,254]$ (highly variable)
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:sys-histogram} shows the distribution of systolic ratios,
strongly skewed toward low values ($\mathrm{sys} < 0.3$ for most polytopes).

\subsubsection*{Confidence assessment}

\paragraph{High confidence.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Implementation correctness}: the \texttt{hk2017} crate
    reproduces seven known literature values (see validation table above),
    including both Lagrangian and symplectic products.
  \item \textbf{Test coverage}: all geometric primitives and the HK2017
    algorithm have property-based tests via \texttt{proptest}, including a
    pruned-vs-unpruned equivalence test for random polytopes up to $F = 8$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Moderate confidence.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Numerical stability}: the algorithm involves floating-point
    comparisons in high-dimensional geometry. Edge cases (near-degenerate
    polytopes, nearly-parallel facets) may accumulate rounding errors.
  \item \textbf{Completeness of search}: the HK2017 algorithm uses a
    branch-and-bound search over piecewise linear orbits. While the theorem
    guarantees that the optimal orbit exists in the search space, our
    implementation's pruning heuristics could theoretically miss the optimum.
    The validation against literature values suggests this is not happening in
    practice.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Low confidence (known limitations).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sample sizes at high $F$}: The dataset has 50 samples per facet
    count for~$F \le 10$, but only 2--20 at $F = 11$--$14$. The per-facet
    statistics at high~$F$ have limited statistical power.
  \item \textbf{Non-Lagrangian polytopes}: our random sampling may under- or
    over-represent polytopes with Lagrangian structure (product polytopes or
    polytopes with Lagrangian 2-faces). The systolic ratio distribution may be
    biased relative to a more carefully chosen sampling distribution.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Key findings}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No random counterexamples}: Among 337 random polytopes with
    $F = 5, \ldots, 14$, all satisfy Viterbo's conjecture. The maximum observed
    systolic ratio is~0.718, far below the Pentagon's~1.047.

  \item \textbf{Viterbo holds for ``most'' polytopes}: The systolic ratio
    distribution is heavily skewed toward low values (median~0.209), suggesting
    that generic polytopes satisfy the conjecture and counterexamples require
    special construction (e.g., Lagrangian products with specific symmetries).

  \item \textbf{Systolic ratio grows with $F$}: The median systolic ratio
    increases from~0.086 at $F = 5$ to~0.35 at $F = 12$. Whether this trend
    continues at higher~$F$ and whether it can reach~1 is an open question.

  \item \textbf{Extended range}: By optimizing the HK2017 implementation
    (Section~\ref{sec:optimization}), we extended the search from~$F \le 10$
    to~$F \le 14$, covering a wider range of polytope complexity without
    discovering new counterexamples.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Methodological contribution.}
This experiment demonstrates a reproducible computational
pipeline for probing symplectic conjectures. The rejection sampling approach
(Section~\ref{sec:rejection-sampling}) provides efficient generation of
diverse polytope datasets, and the \texttt{hk2017} crate provides a validated
implementation of the capacity algorithm. This infrastructure can be extended
to more sophisticated sampling strategies and higher facet counts.
