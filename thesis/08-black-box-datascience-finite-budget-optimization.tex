\subsection{Finite-budget local optimization at ten facets}
\label{subsec:black-box-datascience-finite-budget-optimization}

The random-table scan may have failed simply because it did not locally refine
its high-valued candidates.  We therefore ask whether standard and
branch-aware local methods can improve random ten-facet polytopes under a
common finite budget.  On \(64\) previously unused matched starts,
four-anchor branch history attained the largest median final value,
\(0.9848\); transition prediction followed at \(0.9816\), and the finite-gap
model at \(0.9772\).  None of the \(64\) runs of any method reached the
recorded threshold \(1\).  Endpoint controls below show why this is neither a
convergence result nor evidence that the endpoints are local maxima.

Every run used the same \(1000\)\,ms measured serial
evaluator-plus-optimizer start-new-work cutoff and a \(128\)-call cap.  Work
begun below the cutoff was retained on completion, with no separate terminal
evaluation: the endpoints share an atomic stopping rule, not identical realized
times.  The starts were disjoint from the development and tuning populations,
and the seven methods were fixed before the held-out outcomes were computed.

The retained runs use one common historical binary64 evaluator: it
reconstructs the polytope and executes its configured legacy orbit search.
Write \(\widehat{\operatorname{sys}}(a)\) for the JSON field called
\texttt{sys} in those traces.  Its candidate-family completeness is untested,
so \(\widehat{\operatorname{sys}}\) is not a certified value of the
mathematical \(\operatorname{sys}\).  Nevertheless, applying the same
evaluator to every proposal on matched starts and charging its measured cost
gives a controlled comparison of the optimizer implementations; uncertainty
in the absolute objective is shared rather than method-specific.

The methods adapt standard gradient, derivative-free, and population ideas
\cite{NocedalWright2006} to the lower-envelope structure
\[
  \operatorname{sys}(a)=\min_\sigma s_\sigma(a).
\]
Two methods follow one currently minimizing branch, two maximize an affine
minimum of several nearby branches, and the four-anchor method additionally
retains candidate words from recent full searches; its transition variant
predicts newly feasible words on longer internal moves.  CMA-ES supplies a
population baseline and coordinate search polls both signs of the \(25\)
symmetry-transverse basis axes.  The ridge \(f(x,y)=y-|x|\) explains the
multi-branch motivation: either selected branch gradient leaves the ridge,
whereas balancing both branches identifies its ascending direction.  The
frozen outer steps are recorded in
Table~\ref{tab:black-box-datascience-f10-optimizer-steps} in the data-science
appendix.

On matched starts, the median advantage of four-anchor history over transition
prediction was \(0.00333\), with bootstrap \(95\%\) interval
\(0.000073\)--\(0.00824\).  Transition prediction had improved the development
population but ranked second on held-out starts.  Relative to four-anchor
history, the other median values were lower by \(0.00762\) for the finite-gap
model, \(0.1034\) for literal branch gradients, \(0.1297\) for safeguarded
branch gradients, \(0.1665\) for CMA-ES, and \(0.3838\) for coordinate search.

The compute curves alone do not establish convergence.  A signed basis poll
found a fully re-evaluated improvement at \(7/16\) one-second branch-history
endpoints; after rerunning the same starts for five seconds, this fell to
\(2/16\).  Moreover, one endpoint that passed the basis poll still admitted a
validated oblique path with gain \(0.00223\) over normalized path length
\(0.01\), ending with tested slope \(0.0620\).  Thus both insufficient compute
and directions missed by a coordinate poll occur in the retained population.

Two controls check whether the branch model can recover known ascent and why
some proposals fail.  At HKO it accepted no move, while it improved all \(16\)
predeclared perturbations and recovered \(93.4\%\)--\(100\%\) of their known
evaluator gap in one step.  At the eight highest tuning endpoints, \(5/8\)
admitted a validated branch-model improvement, but \(52/80\) proposed moves
decreased the recomputed evaluator value despite a represented target winner
and positive affine prediction.  A selected three-proposal audit found that
the two failures crossed a zero eigenvalue after KKT perturbations \(218\) and
\(67\) times the smallest source eigenvalue magnitude; the positive control
had ratio \(1.53\) and did not cross zero.  The detailed calibration and audit
are in the data-science appendix.

These controls have deliberately limited scopes: the HKO directions form a
four-direction development panel, the endpoint poll uses selected axes and
radii, and the KKT audit contains two failures and one positive control.
Consequently they diagnose observed failures but estimate neither a
population miss rate nor a universal trust cutoff.

We therefore select four-anchor branch history only as the best of the seven
tested implementations for this random \(F=10\) population and one-second
budget.  The ranking does not transfer without new evidence to another facet
count, starting distribution, budget, or certified capacity evaluator, and
the experiment does not establish convergence or local maximality.  It does
show that local refinement raises many random starts close to the evaluator
threshold, while ordinary insufficient depth, missed oblique ascent, and
ill-conditioned finite-step branch models all remain observable obstacles.
