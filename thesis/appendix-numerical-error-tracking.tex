% How the numerical algorithm reports results: certified vs uncertain
% candidates, error intervals, and when the answer is conclusive.
%
% Status: draft, not yet Jörn-reviewed.

After evaluating all candidate pairs $(S, \sigma)$,
the numerical algorithm must report a capacity value with
appropriate certainty qualifiers.
Each candidate is classified by two independent criteria:
its \emph{predicate status} (whether all three-valued
predicates returned $\textsc{true}$, or some returned
$\textsc{unknown}$) and its \emph{numerical stability}
(whether the KKT system was well-conditioned or
near-singular).

\paragraph{Candidate classification.}
A candidate $(S, \sigma)$ with computed value~$\hat Q$ falls
into one of three categories:
\begin{description}
\item[Certified.]
  All predicates are $\textsc{true}$
  and the system is well-conditioned.
  The computed~$\hat Q$ is the value of a genuinely
  feasible critical point, with negligible rounding error.
\item[Uncertain (predicate).]
  Some predicate returned $\textsc{unknown}$
  (typically a $\beta_i$ near zero).
  The pair may or may not satisfy the exact constraints,
  so~$\hat Q$ may come from an inadmissible point.
\item[Uncertain (numerical).]
  All predicates are $\textsc{true}$ or $\textsc{unknown}$,
  but the system is near-singular
  (small $\sigma_{\min}$ but $\tilde P_{\text{dismiss}} \neq
  \textsc{true}$, so the node was not dismissed).
  The computed~$\hat Q$ carries an error interval
  $[\hat Q - \delta, \hat Q + \delta]$
  where $\delta$ reflects the amplified solve error
  from the large condition number.
\end{description}
A candidate can be uncertain for both reasons simultaneously.

\paragraph{Final answer.}
Let $Q_{\text{cert}}$ be the largest value among certified
candidates, and let $[Q_{\text{unc}}^-, Q_{\text{unc}}^+]$
be the interval containing the largest uncertain candidate
(accounting for both predicate and numerical uncertainty).
If no certified candidate exists (all candidates are uncertain),
the algorithm can only report the uncertain interval
$[(2\, Q_{\text{unc}}^+)^{-1},\; \infty)$ and flag the result
for investigation.
Otherwise, three cases arise:
\begin{enumerate}
\item
  $Q_{\text{cert}} \geq Q_{\text{unc}}^+$:
  the certified candidate dominates.
  The capacity is
  $c_{\mathrm{EHZ}}(K) = 1 / (2\, Q_{\text{cert}})$
  with high confidence.
\item
  $Q_{\text{cert}} < Q_{\text{unc}}^+$
  but $Q_{\text{cert}} \geq Q_{\text{unc}}^-$:
  the uncertain candidate \emph{might} beat the certified one.
  The algorithm reports both values:
  \[
    c_{\mathrm{EHZ}}(K) \in
    \bigl[
      (2\, Q_{\text{unc}}^+)^{-1},\;
      (2\, Q_{\text{cert}})^{-1}
    \bigr]
  \]
  (the capacity lies in this interval, with the lower bound
  from the uncertain candidate and the upper bound from the
  certified one, since higher~$Q$ gives lower capacity).
\item
  $Q_{\text{cert}} < Q_{\text{unc}}^-$:
  the uncertain candidate strictly dominates.
  The capacity depends on whether the uncertain candidate is
  truly admissible.
  The algorithm flags this case for investigation.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Empirical error tracking]\label{rem:empirical-errors}
In practice, we track error bounds empirically in the Rust
implementation rather than deriving tight analytic bounds
for every operation.
The SVD decomposition accounts for roughly 80\% of the
computation time; the numerical estimation and error
propagation happen \emph{after} the SVD and do not
require modifying the SVD computation itself.
The implementation can accumulate error contributions through
a dedicated data structure that tracks the best certified
candidate and the best uncertain candidate separately,
evaluating the error bound from
Proposition~\ref{prop:approximate-dismissal} for each
near-singular system encountered.
This makes the error tracking essentially free in terms of
additional computational cost.
\end{remark}
