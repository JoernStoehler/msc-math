                      Probing Viterbo’s Conjecture
                                       Jörn Stöhler

                                            2026


                                          Abstract
    Haim–Kislev and Ostrover disproved Viterbo’s conjecture with a Lagrangian product
of two regular pentagons. We investigate the geometry near their example and use finite
computation to look for further structure in the systolic ratio of four-dimensional convex
polytopes.
    Our main theorem shows that the HKO body is a local maximizer among polytopes with
exactly ten facets. Nearby equality occurs precisely under translations, positive dilations
and linear symplectic transformations.
    We implement, optimize and extend two capacity algorithms, based on the quadratic
program of Haim–Kislev and the flow graph of Chaidez and Hutchings. We also study the
first-order variation of capacity. As a side theorem, we prove an explicit capacity formula for
the relative-rotation family of two regular pentagons. The proof uses an exhaustive exact
computation and shows that the HKO body is a global maximizer of the systolic ratio in
this family.
    An empirical investigation looks for statistical patterns in geometric measurements of
sampled polytopes, with the aim of turning those patterns into mathematical conjectures.
We find patterns among bodies with systolic ratio below one, but do not establish that these
patterns extend to the regime above one. This leaves open whether further progress requires
different statistical methods or new geometrically motivated measurements.

1     Introduction
How large can a convex body’s symplectic capacity be if its volume is fixed? Viterbo’s conjecture
proposed that a ball gives the largest value [Vit00]. For the Ekeland–Hofer–Zehnder capacity in
dimension four, the proposed inequality was

                                     sys(K) :=              ≤ 1.
                                                 2 vol4 (K)
The quantity on the left is called the systolic ratio. Capacity scales by the square of a dilation
and four-dimensional volume by its fourth power, so this ratio compares shape independently of
overall size. Translations and linear symplectic maps also preserve it.
     The inequality is false. Haim–Kislev and Ostrover found a counterexample formed from two
regular pentagons in complementary Lagrangian planes [HO26]. Writing the coordinates as
(q, p) ∈ R2 ⊕ R2 , one representative is

                    KHKO = P5 × R(−π/2)P5 ,          sys(KHKO ) =          > 1.

Here P5 is a centred regular pentagon with a vertex on the positive horizontal axis, and R(θ) is
planar rotation. A product placed in the q- and p-planes is called a Lagrangian product. The
relative position of its factors affects capacity even though it does not affect volume.
    The counterexample leaves several geometric questions. Is the HKO body a local maximum
of the systolic ratio among convex bodies? Are there other families with large ratios? Can the
computations reveal geometric relationships involving capacity? This thesis investigates these
questions through local analysis, finite algorithms and data science. This is the sense in which
we continue to probe Viterbo’s conjecture: although its proposed bound is false, the geometric
relationship between symplectic capacity and volume remains to be understood.
    The contributions grew out of a broad computational investigation. We developed methods
for evaluating capacity and used them to explore examples, following up on questions that the
computations suggested. This led to several separate results, from a local maximality theorem
to algorithms and statistical observations. The following overview explains what each part
contributes to that investigation.

1.1    Capacities, dynamics and convex geometry
Symplectic capacities measure constraints on embeddings that volume alone cannot detect. Two
basic examples are the Gromov width cG , defined by the largest symplectic ball that embeds
into a domain, and the cylindrical capacity cZ , defined by the smallest symplectic cylinder
into which the domain embeds. With the usual normalization, every symplectic capacity lies
between these two [Edt24]. The EHZ capacity has a different description: on convex bodies it
is the least action of a closed characteristic. Thus comparing capacities connects an embedding
question to a question about Hamiltonian trajectories.
    The conjecture that all normalized capacities agree on convex domains was stronger than
Viterbo’s volume bound. Indeed, the Gromov width satisfies that bound directly by volume
preservation. The HKO example therefore also separates the EHZ capacity from the Gromov
width [HO26]. The equality of EHZ and cylindrical capacity on general convex domains in
four dimensions is still listed as open in [HO26, Remark 1.4(v)]. Edtmair instead characterizes
cylindrical capacity on smooth star-shaped domains in R4 with dynamically convex boundary
by the least area of a disk-like global surface of section, and proves coincidence of all normalized
capacities for convex domains sufficiently C 3 -close to the ball [Edt24]. A global surface of section
is a surface whose interior is transverse to the flow and is met in both forward and backward
time by every trajectory outside its boundary. These results illustrate how information about
the flow can determine an embedding obstruction.

                                                  5
   There is also a direct connection to a central problem in convex geometry. For a centrally
symmetric convex body L ⊂ Rn and its polar L◦ , Artstein–Avidan, Karasev and Ostrover prove
cEHZ (L × L◦ ) = 4 [AKO14]. Viterbo’s proposed inequality on these particular products is
consequently equivalent to the lower bound vol(L) vol(L◦ ) ≥ 4n /n! in the symmetric Mahler
conjecture. The general counterexample does not invalidate this equivalence for the restricted
family. Capacity–volume questions therefore retain significance beyond the original universal
bound.
   Local maximality has a dynamical precedent as well. Abbondandolo and Benedetti prove
that Zoll contact forms, whose Reeb orbits are all closed with the same minimal period, locally
maximize the contact systolic ratio [AB23]. Their results include the volume–capacity inequality
near the ball. The HKO body presents a different setting: it has corners and a ratio larger
than the ball’s. Our local theorem studies how such a nonsmooth example behaves under
perturbations of its supporting facets.

1.2   A local theorem around the counterexample
Our main result partially answers the first question: every sufficiently Hausdorff-close ten-facet
polytope has ratio at most that of KHKO . In a smaller neighborhood, equality occurs precisely
for translations, positive dilations and linear symplectic images of KHKO ; see Theorem 7.1.
Thus a small change of shape that is not one of these symmetries lowers the ratio. Allowing
facets to appear or disappear is a different local problem, which the theorem leaves open.
    The proof uses upper bounds that agree with the systolic ratio at HKO. For each nearby
direction, at least one of these bounds decreases. It follows that the ratio itself decreases. The
finite task is to construct enough such bounds and check their derivatives. Feasible weights
in Haim–Kislev’s capacity formula provide the bounds. An algebraic computation establishes
the derivative conditions; the proof explains why those conditions imply the geometric local
maximum.

1.3   From boundary dynamics to finite calculations
For a smooth convex body, the EHZ capacity is the least action of a closed characteristic on
its boundary. Polytopes have corners, so the relevant curves are generalized characteristics.
Their velocities are governed by the normals of the facets they meet. Haim–Kislev’s theorem
makes the capacity computable by a finite optimization over facet orders and nonnegative
weights [Hai19].
    We expand the proof in Haim–Kislev’s original paper into a self-contained presentation for
a master’s-level reader. We explain the dual action principle and the existence of a minimizing
curve with a finite list of distinct facet directions.
    For Lagrangian products, we derive a further reduction from the bilinear structure of the
objective: some maximizing word uses at most three facets from each factor. This gives a
smaller finite search for the capacity, without classifying every minimizing orbit.
    We pursued the flow-graph method of Chaidez and Hutchings [CH21] as an alternative
way to compute capacity. Its performance in our tests made it less useful for large searches,
but it provides a reference implementation for cross-checking the quadratic-program method.
Agreement between the two gives additional evidence that the implementations compute the
intended quantity.

1.4   Looking for mathematical structure in data
We also use data science to seek geometric relationships involving capacity. The aim is to
discover statistical patterns, interpret them geometrically, and turn them into conjectures that
can be proved or refuted. The experiments find patterns below systolic ratio one, but do not
establish their extension above one. One geometric interpretation yields an exact edge–width
formula for a measured quantity on Lagrangian products; relating it to capacity remains open.

1.5   How to read the thesis
The order follows the passage from geometry to computation and then to applications. Sections
2–4 explain how nonsmooth boundary dynamics become a finite quadratic program. Section 5
presents the alternative flow-graph method, and Section 6 develops first-order variation. These
foundations prepare the local theorem in Section 7 and the further investigations in Sections 8
and 9.
    A reader primarily interested in the HKO theorem can start with its statement and proof
strategy in Section 7, returning to Sections 2–4 for the capacity formula and to Section 6
for variation. The flow-graph and data-science chapters are not prerequisites for that proof.
Section 8 describes the empirical investigation and can be read independently of the HKO
certificate; Section 9 gives the separate theorem for rotated pentagons. The later sections
document visualizations, numerical methods, code and data, and reflect on the use of AI. The
appendices contain computational detail that can be consulted when checking or reproducing
the results.

    All convex bodies below are compact convex subsets of R4 with non-empty interior. Unless
stated otherwise, the origin lies in the interior.

2.3   EHZ capacity
For smooth convex bodies, the Ekeland–Hofer and Hofer–Zehnder capacities coincide. Their
common value is the minimum action among closed characteristics on the boundary. We use
the resulting EHZ capacity in the form

                  cEHZ (K) = min{A(γ) : γ is a closed characteristic on ∂K}.                   (4)

This is the smooth least-action characterization of [AO14, Theorem 2.2]. Equivalently, the mini-
mum may be taken over Reeb orbits. The contact normalization only chooses the parametrization
of each characteristic, while the action is a line integral and is invariant under orientation-
preserving reparametrization. For a contact-normalized representative, A(γ) is also the period.
The same least-action theorem gives a positive attained minimum.
    For a nonsmooth convex body, we define capacity as the limit of the capacities of smooth
convex approximations in the Hausdorff metric. This limit is independent of the approximating

sequence. The least-action description remains valid when closed characteristics are replaced
by the generalized characteristics of Definition 2.1 [AO14, Proposition 2.7]. Their finite facet
description on a polytope is derived in Section 3.

under symplectomorphisms, and conformal:

Definition 2.2 (Systolic-ratio symmetries). Let

                      Gsys = {x 7→ b + ρM x : b ∈ R4 , ρ > 0, M ∈ Sp(4, R)}.

                                          sys(gK) = sys(K).

3     Generalized Reeb Orbits On Polytopes
The boundary of a polytope is not smooth, so the smooth Reeb vector field is replaced by
a differential inclusion. The inclusion still has contact normalization, but it allows convex
combinations of several facet directions at a nonsmooth face. The purpose of this section is to
show why the capacity problem nevertheless contains one minimizer described by finitely many
pure facet directions. It first separates the finite velocity data from the additional base-point
condition that places the path on the prescribed facets, and then proves the simple-minimizer
theorem that supplies the input to the Haim–Kislev quadratic program.

4    The Haim–Kislev Quadratic Program

optimum by solving linear stationarity systems and comparing their values. Second, it proves
reductions for Lagrangian products of planar polygons. Those reductions make the later family
calculations practical.

Qmax is the largest objective value among the feasible words in this candidate family.

to a vertex of the closure polytope need not preserve or improve it.

degeneracy can only lower the rank used in the support bound.

4.5    Use in later certificates
The later computer-assisted proofs use these results in two ways. The HKO local-maximum
proof uses selected active words to build smooth upper bounds. The rotated-pentagon proof uses
the specialized Lagrangian-product enumeration from Theorem 4.4, then checks the algebraic
inequalities needed to compare every surviving branch with the displayed one.
    The numerical implementation follows the same distinction between general polytopes and
products. In the general case it examines the candidate words and their stationary points, using
proved exclusion criteria to reduce the search. It returns an interval containing the capacity.
For a recognized Lagrangian product it instead enumerates the closure-polytope vertices from
Algorithm 4.7 and compares the resulting objective values. This route returns the exact
capacity of the supplied input, whose floating-point coordinates are treated as exact rational
numbers. Both routes use interval bounds to control numerical error and exact arithmetic when
a comparison cannot otherwise be decided. Section 11 describes those checks. The HKO and
rotated-pentagon proofs use separate SageMath programs over the relevant number fields.
    The QP is therefore the appropriate language when the global objective value, its competing
branches, or inequalities between them matter. The next section keeps the same simple facet
words but asks a different geometric question: it propagates actual breakpoints through facet-
pair sections and detects a closed orbit as a fixed point of an affine return map. This second
route is conditional on explicit regularity assumptions, but it exposes the trajectory geometry
and provides an independent implementation comparison.

5     Computing closed trajectories with a flow graph
The quadratic program computes capacity by comparing possible facet orders and weights. A
second approach follows trajectories through the facets themselves. In the relative interior
of a facet, the contact-normalized velocity is constant. Following a chosen facet direction
carries a point on an incoming two-dimensional section to an outgoing section by an affine map.
Composing these maps around a facet word produces a return map; a fixed point gives a closed
trajectory.
    We use the local passage geometry of Chaidez and Hutchings [CH21]. The exhaustive search
below is justified by the simple-minimizer theorem: some capacity-minimizing trajectory uses

We now state the regularity boundary of the algorithm.

Definition 5.2 (Flow-graph regular presentation). The facet presentation above is flow-graph
regular if:

   1. for distinct i, j, Fi ∩ Fj ̸= ∅ implies ω0 (ai , aj ) ̸= 0;

      Φw satisfies det(I − Mw ) ̸= 0.

The last condition is called finite-orbit regularity.

Algorithm 5.3 (Idealized exhaustive flow-graph search). Enumerate every simple cyclic facet
word once. For each word:

   1. discard it if an adjacent facet intersection is empty or an adjacent transition sign is not
      positive;

   2. discard it if its length is at most four: under the regularity hypothesis, these facet directions
      are linearly independent and cannot form a closed positive-time path;

   3. construct its closed tube (Pw , Φw , Aw ); discard the word if Pw = ∅, and otherwise solve
      Φw (u) = u;

   4. reject a singular fixed-point equation as outside the regular case, and otherwise record the
      unique solution only if it lies in Pw and every reconstructed segment time is positive.

Return the least positive recorded action.

5.3    Why the regular class is generic

count there are only finitely many of them. Pairwise symplectic degeneracy and short-word
dependence are ordinary polynomial conditions. The only non-immediate point is that the
long-word determinant is not the zero rational function.

5.4   Relation to CH2021
Chaidez–Hutchings study a finite model that compares Reeb dynamics on a four-dimensional
polytope with Reeb dynamics on its smoothings [CH21]. Their setting and proof route differ from
the general Haim–Kislev formula. They assume that the polytope is symplectic; in particular,
its two-faces are not Lagrangian, so the relevant combinatorial Reeb direction on each two-face
is one-dimensional.
    A Chaidez–Hutchings combinatorial Reeb orbit is a cyclic sequence of oriented line segments
in the boundary. Their symplectic flow graph records the orbits which avoid the one-skeleton:
its vertices are open two-faces, an edge is a Reeb passage through a three-face, and periodic
graph orbits correspond to these Type 1 combinatorial Reeb orbits with the same action [CH21,
Proposition 2.14]. Their smoothing theory relates this model to Reeb orbits on smooth convex
approximations and yields a capacity formula involving Type 1 and Type 2 combinatorial orbits
[CH21, Theorems 1.11 and 1.12, Corollary 1.15].
    The rational-arithmetic algorithm developed here uses part of the same local geometry but
a different global argument. Its nodes are closed facet-pair sections, and it searches simple
facet words whose affine return maps have fixed points. The simple-minimizer theorem supplies
completeness on the stated regular class. Consequently this algorithm neither implements
the CH2021 rotation pruning nor imports the Type 1/Type 2/Type 3 classification as its
capacity proof. In particular, an action cutoff alone would not control arbitrarily long helical
combinatorial words in the CH2021 model; the finite search here instead comes from simple
words with no repeated facet direction.

5.5    Rational implementation and evidence
The current executable implementation targets Corollary 5.5: it represents the normals and all
tube arithmetic by rational numbers. It enumerates transition-pruned simple directed cycles,
checks the short-word independence hypothesis, constructs and composes affine tubes, solves
fixed-point equations in rational arithmetic, and accepts an orbit only after reconstructing
strictly positive segment times.
     This implementation does not validate an arbitrary raw halfspace description. It assumes
that the caller supplies a bounded irredundant presentation in dimension four, together with
matching rational matrices for facet intersections and symplectic signs. It rejects a caller-marked
nonempty distinct facet pair with zero symplectic pairing, failure of the short-word independence
hypothesis, and unsupported singular fixed sets. An inconsistent singular fixed equation, or one
whose fixed set misses the tube, is instead a certified no-orbit outcome. Thus the implementation
is intended to realize the corollary on rational inputs satisfying both these input requirements
and the regularity hypotheses; correspondence is tested, not formally verified.
     The earlier binary64 flow-graph prototype was approximate and lacked a sound way to
distinguish predicates proved true, proved false, or left undecided. It was retired and supplies
no current flow-graph capacity evidence.
     On selected deterministic rational F5, F6, and F7 cases, rational flow-graph search agrees
with the certified Haim–Kislev/QP scalar aggregate. These comparisons, the retained words,
and the targeted resolver tests are implementation evidence for the stated rational inputs; they
do not prove the idealized algorithm or the CH2021 capacity scope. Targeted tests also exercise
tube construction, fixed-point reconstruction, and rejection of unsupported inputs. These checks
are falsification and correspondence evidence on selected examples; they do not prove tube
semantics or broad input coverage.


6     First-Order Variation in Facet Coordinates
We now let the supporting hyperplanes move and ask how the systolic ratio changes. The
volume has a direct derivative in a neighborhood with fixed simple face structure. Capacity is
more delicate: the Haim–Kislev formula expresses it through a maximum over words, and the
maximizing word can change.
    There are two useful calculations. A smooth feasible choice of weights gives a differentiable
upper bound for the ratio. If the constrained optimizer is nondegenerate, its weights also vary
smoothly, and we can differentiate its optimized value. The first construction needs less: it will
supply the upper bounds in the HKO proof even at singular stationarity systems. For local
search, the second construction gives branch gradients that can be compared before proposing
a step.

6.5   The non-generic boundary
The smooth branch calculation can break down in four ways.

  • If a maximizing weight is zero, deleting that coordinate is valid for the capacity value
    at the base point. It need not preserve the nearby slope: the longer word may acquire a
    positive weight on one side of the perturbation and define a different branch there.

  • If the constraint rank drops or the KKT matrix is singular, the implicit function theorem
    does not provide a unique smooth optimizing branch. A continuum of optimizers may
    nevertheless have a well-defined value.

  • A tie is described by (32) only when all relevant tied branches extend smoothly and the
    stated coverage and gap hypotheses hold. Base-point branch enumeration alone does not
    prove this.

  • When facet labels become redundant or the face combinatorics changes, both the branch
    family and the smooth volume formula require a new local chart. Hausdorff continuity of
    capacity and volume across that boundary does not imply differentiability there.

    All constraints and objectives are semialgebraic. A possible route to a fully general one-
sided theory would organize germs of feasible optimizing branches by a finite decomposition
of direction space. Such a construction would have to record support appearances, singular
optimizer sets, and dominance of every discarded germ. Obtaining a complete first-order
description at an arbitrary non-generic polytope remains open here.

6.6   Application at HKO
The HKO argument uses the feasible-section calculation. It constructs twenty-six upper bounds
that agree with the true ratio at the base body. Together their derivatives force at least
one bound to decrease in every direction transverse to the symmetries. Section 7 gives the
construction and proves that this directional statement yields a strict local maximum. No
continuation of an optimizing branch is required.

6.7   Implementation and validation status
The implementation evaluates the branch and volume formulas in dual-vertex coordinates.
Regression tests compare the volume derivative with central finite differences and compare
a fixed-word capacity derivative with central finite differences on known fixtures. An exact
rational implementation of the capacity-gradient formula is also compared with the floating-
point formula on a rational simplex fixture. These checks test the correspondence between the
formulas and their implementations; they do not certify branch coverage, finite-step prediction,
or local maximality.
    For the HKO theorem, the evidence standard is stronger and different. The SageMath verifier

The ten supporting hyperplanes may move in all four coordinate directions, so the nearby bodies

7.2   Local coordinates and volume
Lemma 7.2 (A simple row neighborhood). There is a neighborhood N of a0 in (R4 )10 on which
K(a) is bounded and simple, with the same vertex–facet incidences as K0 . Every sufficiently
Hausdorff-close ten-facet polytope has a labelled presentation with a ∈ N , and its rows tend to
a0 as the body tends to K0 .
Proof. The origin stays in the interior under sufficiently small Hausdorff perturbations of
K0 = K(a0 ). One way to see this is through support functions: hK (u) = maxx∈K u · x. Write
B for the Euclidean unit ball. If rB ⊂ K0 and the Hausdorff distance from K to K0 is less than
ϵ < r, then hK (u) ≥ hK0 (u) − ϵ ≥ r − ϵ for every unit vector u. Thus (r − ϵ)B ⊂ K. On such a
neighborhood polarity is continuous: the radial function, the distance from zero to the boundary
along a unit direction, of K ◦ is 1/hK on the unit sphere, and the common positive lower bound
on hK makes these reciprocals converge uniformly. The polars are uniformly bounded convex
bodies, so this also gives Hausdorff convergence.
    The polar K0◦ has the ten vertices a0,i . Choose disjoint small balls about them. Each vertex
is uniquely exposed by some linear functional ℓi . For polars sufficiently close to K0◦ , every
maximizer of ℓi lies in the corresponding ball. Otherwise a convergent sequence of maximizers
outside that ball would limit to a maximizer on K0◦ different from a0,i . A maximizing face
contains a vertex. A polytope K with exactly ten facets has a polar with exactly ten vertices;
hence there is exactly one polar vertex in each ball and none elsewhere. Labelling these vertices
ai gives
               K = {x : ai · x ≤ 1, i = 1, . . . , 10},  ai −→ a0,i as K −→ K0 .

    For completeness, the face structure is stable in this neighbourhood. The base body is
a product of two polygons and is simple: precisely four facets meet at each vertex, with
independent normals. Their four equations therefore determine a smoothly varying intersection
under a small change of a. All the remaining inequalities have strict slack at that intersection,
so it remains a vertex with exactly those incident facets.
    No new vertex can appear. The convex hull of the base normals contains a ball about zero;
the convex hull of sufficiently close normals contains a smaller common ball. Consequently the
bodies K(a) are uniformly bounded. If arbitrarily close parameters had a new vertex, choose
four independent active constraints there. There are finitely many four-element subsets, so
along a subsequence the same subset is active and the vertices converge to a point of K0 where
those four constraints are equalities. Simplicity forces them to be the incident facets of one of
the original vertices. Their intersection is the continuation already constructed, contradicting
newness. This argument also excludes a new vertex arising from a subset whose normals are
dependent at the base point; no invertibility assumption for such a subset is needed.
    The vertex–facet incidences therefore remain unchanged. In particular each inequality still
defines a facet. Conversely, small changes of the ten normals give Hausdorff-small changes of
K(a): their convex hulls converge, contain a common ball about zero, and polarity is continuous
there.

8      Finding mathematical patterns with data science
We use data science to look for geometric relationships involving symplectic capacity. We sample
random polytopes and compute a broad range of readily available geometric quantities, including
symplectic invariants and measurements that can change under symplectic transformations.
The sampling distributions are specified in Section 8.1.


                                                         48
    We then look for simple statistical patterns without requiring a geometric explanation in
advance. When a pattern emerges, we return to the definitions and try to understand why the
quantities might be related. This can suggest a tentative conjecture, which further examples
may help sharpen or refute. The aim is to formulate a precise statement and find a proof that
explains the observed relationship.
    The experiments reported below carried out parts of this program. They found a strong
association between symplectic ridge area and the numerical systolic ratio. Examining the
descriptor geometrically clarified what it measures, but did not establish a general relation
to capacity. The pattern also served a complementary aim: finding bodies with large ratios.
Selection rules based on it improved the results of fresh sampling before we had an explanation
for their success. The chapter follows this signal from its statistical discovery through geometric
interpretation and search tests, then considers search methods that change the bodies themselves.

8.5   Testing whether a pattern can guide fresh selection
Alongside geometric interpretation, we tested the signal as a search rule. A predictor can
describe an existing dataset without helping on new bodies. We therefore changed the order
of computation: generate fresh geometry, compute its descriptors, fix the selected bodies and
comparison bodies, and only then evaluate their ratios. Selection cannot use the target values
at this stage.
    An initial pool of 100,000 product candidates tested thirty scalar selection sets. These
included low ridge sums and other ridge summaries, as well as high face counts, selecting globally
or separately within side-count groups. For example, the per-group low-sum rule selected the ten
smallest values of S in each group. The union of all selections and their baselines required 1,675
evaluations, not 100,000. Its maximum recorded ratio was 0.867546. Several rules selected the
same bodies; their outcomes cannot be added as independent repetitions. The next experiments
isolated more specific rules.
    The concentration experiment generated a new pool of 100,000 candidates. Within each
side-count group, it first retained the lowest one percent by S. It then divided this tail into
two equally sized parts according to C. The question was whether spreading the already small
total symplectic area more evenly would select better bodies. The half with smaller C had a
mean recorded ratio larger by 0.0340 than the other half. The difference was positive in eight of
the ten groups. This met the descriptive criterion fixed before evaluation: a positive aggregate
difference and positive differences in at least seven groups. The run evaluated 2,490 bodies and
reached a maximum of 0.902384. The criterion was a continuation rule for this experiment, not
a statistical significance test.

8.6   Transfer and the limit of a stronger filter
The ridge observation first came from a mixture of generic polytopes and products. To test a
concrete selection rule on generic ten-facet bodies, we generated 10,000 fresh candidates and
selected the lowest one percent by mean normalized ridge area, S(K) divided by the ridge count.
Generic bodies with the same facet count can have different ridge counts, so this differs from
selecting by the sum. The 100 selected bodies had mean recorded ratio 0.626, compared with
0.339 for 100 disjoint controls. The difference was 0.287, with a percentile-bootstrap interval
[0.250, 0.324] from 20,000 resamples of the realized panels.
    The same experiment asked whether a more extreme filter would help further. The ten
bodies in the lowest 0.1% were compared with the other ninety selected bodies. Their mean
difference was −0.0318, with interval [−0.0946, 0.0331]. This failed the criterion for expanding
that particular search to a larger pool. It did not establish that stronger filtering is harmful:
the interval permits effects of either sign. The useful contrast is that a coarse geometric filter
worked well while the next restriction did not show an additional gain.
    We also changed the product sampling law. Starting from the original random normals and
heights, we replaced every support height in both factors by one. Each side of the resulting
polygon lies on a tangent line to the unit circle. We required both the original factors and the
changed factors to be admissible, and then separately normalized the two changed factors to
area one. This produces a conditioned family of tangential polygons.
    On one fresh pool with 3,200 eligible bodies in each of the 4 × 6 and 6 × 6 groups, ridge
and covariance selection were compared with shared, disjoint controls. Each selection had
sixteen memberships per group; five selected bodies overlapped, giving 91 actual evaluations.
Averaging the two group effects equally, relative to controls, covariance selection gained 0.259
and ridge selection gained 0.225. Their respective resampling intervals were [0.192, 0.329] and
[0.160, 0.296]. These intervals condition on the realized panels; one seed and two side-count
groups do not measure variation across arbitrary source laws. The shared controls also make
the comparisons dependent. The experiment nevertheless gives a concrete instance in which
both rules remained useful after changing how bodies were generated.

8.8   Adapting the sampling distribution
Fresh sampling won the rotation comparison, but it repeatedly draws from the same law.
We next tested whether the evaluated bodies could improve that law. The cross-entropy
method [Boe+05] used here repeatedly fits a simple distribution to the best part of a population,
then samples another population from it.
    This experiment used 5 × 5 products. We described each pentagon by the cyclic gaps
between its five normal angles and the relative lengths of its five dual normals. There are four
independent angular-gap coordinates and four independent logarithmic lengths per factor. One
relative angle between the factors gives seventeen coordinates altogether. Separate factor scales
and a simultaneous planar rotation do not change the mathematical ratio, which allows their
removal from this description. The precise coordinates and update are given below so that
adapting the distribution has a concrete meaning.
    Both arms began with the same 64 independent products from the original angle-and-height
source. The adaptive arm fitted a diagonal Gaussian in these coordinates, then moved its mean
and coordinate variances halfway toward those of the sixteen bodies with largest numerical
ratio. It drew another 64 bodies, evaluated them, and repeated this update. Each arm had four
populations including initialization, for 256 charged evaluations. The comparison arm continued
independent sampling throughout. A variance floor kept the adaptive sampler from eliminating
a coordinate’s variation entirely.

8.9   Local search from matched starting bodies
The adaptive sampler changes a distribution over products. A separate, earlier experiment
changed the dual normals of generic ten-facet bodies directly. Seven fixed implementations
started from the same 64 bodies. Each run allowed at most 128 charged full evaluations and a
one-second accumulated evaluator-plus-optimizer time ceiling, excluding the initial evaluation.
Invalid proposals still consumed calls. Work begun below the time ceiling was allowed to finish,
so terminal results can include a small overrun; they are not values available at exactly one
second.
    The capacity calculation suggests local branch functions, each associated with a particular
candidate in the variational problem. When several smooth functions compete through their
minimum, following the gradient of one selected function can fail to increase the minimum.
For example, the minimum of x and −x decreases in both directions away from zero, although
either individual function has a direction of increase. This motivates methods that include more
than the currently selected branch.
    The literal gradient implementation took a fixed step along that branch’s gradient and
accepted usable evaluations even when the ratio decreased. The safeguarded version removed the
symmetry directions, normalized the gradient, and accepted only improvements; its relative step
length expanded by 1.25 after success and contracted by 0.5 after failure. A finite-gap model
included branches whose actions were within ten percent of the minimum and maximized the
minimum of their affine predictions in a small ball. Its proposal still required a full evaluation
and a strict improvement. The finite branch list is a local model, not a complete formula for all
branches near the body.
    Two history methods retained candidates from the current and up to three previous accepted
anchors, then used this larger list for two inner model steps before full evaluation. One also
predicted additional candidates from branch transitions for sufficiently long steps. Their outer
rule accepted any usable evaluation, so a move could reduce the objective even while the
best value seen so far stayed unchanged. The remaining methods were a Gaussian population
search with covariance adaptation (CMA-ES) [Han16], and a poll of both signs of a transverse
coordinate basis, keeping the best improvement.
    Most methods used the complement of the fifteen translation, scaling and linear symplectic
tangent directions. This leaves twenty-five directions at a generic ten-facet body. The coordinate
poll and local model methods rebuilt their transverse space at their anchors; CMA-ES used its
initial space, and the literal gradient used the ambient coordinates. These are choices in the
tested implementations, not interchangeable realizations of one global quotient chart.

8.10     What a failed local probe tells us
A large terminal value also raises a geometric question: has search arrived at a local maximum?
Evaluating a finite set of nearby points can discover an improvement, but failing to find one does
not prove local maximality. The endpoint diagnostics gave concrete examples of this distinction.
    Sixteen endpoints of the history method were chosen across the observed performance range
of the common starts. At each, we evaluated both signs of twenty-five transverse basis directions
at relative radii 10−3 , 10−4 and 10−5 : 150 probes per endpoint. Seven endpoints had at least
one raw positive change and nine had none. The sixteen endpoints were selected by outcome
ranks, so these fractions do not estimate a population convergence probability.
    After allowing a larger search budget on those starts, a further continuation experiment
examined three selected optimizer endpoints and HKO. It tested branch-model and selected-
gradient proposals at five decreasing step lengths, falling back to a signed basis poll, and allowed
at most ten accepted improvements. All three optimizer endpoints took ten improving steps.
One had previously decreased along every tested signed basis direction, yet improved by 0.00223
along a sequence of oblique moves with total normalized path length 0.01. HKO accepted
no move. The theorem establishes HKO’s local status; the finite search supplies a control
observation consistent with it.
    Another fixed suite probed a regular triangle–hexagon product, a rotated square–square
product and the CH2021 example, together with a regular pentagon product chosen on a known
increasing rotation path. It combined signed transverse basis directions, projected random
directions and prescribed rotations at three radii. All 1,482 probes were valid. None of the three
target bodies had a nominal gain above 10−12 , while the pentagon control had twelve, all in the
rotation probes. Its generic basis and random probes found none. Even this control therefore
shows that a finite collection of generic directions can miss an available increase. The target
misses remain numerical diagnostics; they do not exclude untested directions or changes in facet
count. The historical run also has incomplete source provenance, and its broad capacity-derived
bounds do not turn the nominal target misses into a proof.

8.11    What the experiments contribute
The exploratory approach found a strong ridge-area pattern, then gave it more concrete meaning
through edge widths and the area–alignment decomposition. That interpretation ruled out
a universal monotonicity claim and left a more restricted capacity relation to be formulated.
The search approach found geometric information useful before capacity evaluation. Low ridge
area and balanced vertex covariance selected larger recorded ratios in fresh pools, and some
of that usefulness survived changes in the generating law. More severe ridge filtering did not
automatically improve selection. Changing individual bodies also had varied effects: equalizing
heights helped on average but harmed some pairs, while prescribed rotations lost to fresh
sampling under the tested call allocation. Adapting the product distribution improved on IID
in three small paired runs.
    The local-search comparison obtained larger recorded values from models that retained
several candidate branches. Its endpoint checks also exposed improving directions missed by
a basis poll. These are distinct experimental results, with different sources and evaluation
contracts. Together they suggest useful ways to continue searching, while leaving the central
challenge of reaching new high-ratio bodies unresolved. The initial sample, prospective selections,
three new pilots and matched-start optimizer runs produced no stored value above one. This
observation excludes the deliberately supplied HKO and pentagon controls; it neither proves
high-ratio bodies absent from the searched families nor shows that the exploration has exhausted
useful ideas.

11     Numerics
The computations in this thesis support two different kinds of claims. Statements used inside the
HKO and rotated-pentagon proofs are checked by separate SageMath packets over rational or
ordered algebraic fields. The ordinary Rust implementation instead computes the EHZ capacity
of a four-dimensional polytope supplied in binary64 coordinates. It uses floating-point arithmetic
for speed, but it does not accept an unverified floating-point sign or ordering decision: every
such decision is enclosed by an outward error bound or resolved in exact rational arithmetic.

11.1    Input and output contracts
A finite binary64 number is a dyadic rational. The numerical capacity contract regards the
supplied numbers as these exact rationals; it does not claim to recover unavailable source
coordinates from which they may have been rounded. Before the QP search, exact geometry
checks establish that
                                K = {x ∈ R4 : ⟨ai , x⟩ ≤ 1}
is bounded, nonempty, full-dimensional, and described without redundant facets. The exact
primal vertices and vertex–facet incidence are then constructed from the dyadic input. Floating-
point filters may discard a subset only when a one-sided bound proves the exact conclusion; an
indeterminate filter result is resolved exactly.
    The production route accepts at most sixteen facets and requires every primal and dual
vertex to have infinity norm in
                                            [10−3 , 103 ].
These bounds are a deliberately narrow applicability policy, not a theorem about all polytopes.
They exclude scales for which the current binary64 enclosures have not been justified and tested.
The general candidate stream is additionally limited to 100 000 cyclic words. Violating any of
these conditions returns an error rather than an uncertified capacity.
    For a general polytope the result is an outward interval [cL , cU ] containing the exact capacity
of the dyadic input. A scalar wrapper returns the midpoint only if this interval certifies the
relative error requested by the caller. For a recognized Lagrangian product the specialized
route returns the exact rational capacity of the dyadic input, together with outward binary64
endpoints for convenient downstream use.

11.3    Complete general and product routes
The general search constructs the exact transition graph, pruned by facet incidence and
symplectic signs, then enumerates its simple cyclic words. Candidate correctness is not obtained
merely by solving every displayed KKT system. A fixed-word maximum must have nonpositive
curvature in every tangent direction. Conversely, a certified vector z ∈ ker Cτ with z T Hτ z > 0
rules out τ . If τ injects into a longer word σ while preserving cyclic order, zero-extending z
preserves both its tangent constraints and positive curvature. Hence σ is ruled out as well.
Processing words by length allows these proved obstructions to skip many later factorizations.
    For every word not excluded in this way, the route uses the verified fixed-word decision
above, with exact fallback for every indeterminate case. The resulting objective intervals
are aggregated outwards over the complete candidate family, and the reciprocal reverses the
endpoints to enclose the capacity. Exact arithmetic is therefore a selective correctness fallback,
not the default arithmetic for every word.
    For a Lagrangian product, the inverse-defect route is correct but frequently indeterminate
and unnecessarily expensive. The specialized implementation uses Algorithm 4.7. It encloses
factor-closure weights and bilinear objectives in floating-point arithmetic, rejects a support or
order only when the intervals prove it cannot win, and resolves ambiguous supports and every
possible winner exactly. Because the final comparison is rational, this route returns the exact
capacity and every tied sparse winner in the closure-vertex candidate family. It intentionally
does not enumerate all minimizing geometric branches.

11.4    Empirical verification and limitations
The general numerical audit compared the floating-point predicates with exact dyadic-rational
arithmetic on 249 KKT systems: ordinary generated systems, scales from 10−2 through 103 ,
and systems approaching singularity down to binary64 coincidence. It found no incorrect
determinate decision and no violation of a β- or Q-radius. Fifteen cases were indeterminate

                                                   66
and were resolved exactly. The largest observed ratios of actual error to certified radius were
0.00149 for β and 0.00081 for Q.
    Complete-route comparisons used 88 words from three generated general polytopes and 1271
words from three product controls. Exact aggregation found no missed capacity and no missed
minimizer class; every exact capacity lay in the returned interval. On the general cases, the
largest difference between the reported midpoint and exact capacity was 2.31 · 10−14 . These
finite comparisons test the implementation and exercise fallback, but the soundness claim rests
on the preceding error bounds and complete-candidate arguments.
    The product audit used 88 ordinary and adversarial products. Across 1835 closure weights
and 173 496 objective intervals it found no interval violation, no incorrect determinate sign, and
no disagreement between the hybrid and complete exact closure-vertex computations. Raw
comparison with zero disagreed with the exact objective sign 23 559 times, mostly at exact
zero or tiny values; the certified predicate returned indeterminate instead. A separate retained
population of 10 240 random products had a literal capacity winner of length six in every case.
This supports the implementation and stress-tests Theorem 4.6; it is not its proof and does not
exclude longer tied minimizers.
    The guarantees above concern the exact dyadic polytope represented by the input. They do
not propagate measurement error or prior rounding uncertainty in the coordinates, and they
apply only within the stated facet, norm, and candidate-count limits. Proof-facing claims about
algebraic parameter families therefore continue to use the separate exact SageMath certificates.

13     AI in the research process
The use of AI in this project changed as the available models became more capable. My
experience was that they supplied increasingly useful research ideas and could carry out longer
tasks with fewer interruptions. This changed what I could delegate. A request could describe a
mathematical or experimental outcome and leave the agent to choose intermediate steps, write
code, inspect the results and revise its approach. The separate disclosure records AI use; here I
discuss what this experience suggests about organizing mathematical work with agents.

13.1    Delegating an incomplete proof strategy
The local maximality proof is the clearest example of how much of a research task could be
delegated. I asked a GPT-5-series model for a proof that the HKO body is a local maximum. I
suggested looking for several upper bounds and somehow showing that together they control
a neighborhood after quotienting out the symmetries. This was only the overall shape of a
strategy. I had not specified a finite certificate or worked out how the upper bounds should be
constructed.
     The model developed essentially the entire argument from that starting point. It identified
a sufficient configuration of twenty-six upper bounds in the twenty-five transverse dimensions.
An initial derivative-based approach had provided only twenty-five nonsingular cases. The
model then used the weights β in the quadratic objective to construct feasible sections: an
invertible 5 × 5 closure minor lets five weights depend smoothly on the facet parameters while
the remaining weights stay fixed. This supplied upper bounds even where the initial approach
failed. It also completed proof steps involving the volume and wrote the SageMath code for the
exact computations.
     The assignment was therefore a conjecture and an incomplete proof idea; the result was
a developed proof together with computational verification code. The derivative conditions
and the form of the finite certificate were part of the model’s contribution, not conditions I
supplied in the request. The useful specification was the mathematical outcome—a proof of
local maximality—while the model had substantial freedom to discover what would establish it.




                                               68
13.2    Matching the check to the claim
The different parts of this thesis require different evidence. In the HKO argument, arithmetic
checks establish a finite statement about explicitly defined matrices and weights. The written
proof still has to identify those matrices with derivatives of upper bounds for the geometric
quantity. It also has to pass from labelled normals to a neighborhood of convex bodies. Omitting
either connection would leave a mathematical gap even if every computed identity were correct.
    For the search experiments, a high observed value answers a different question from a proof
of a local maximum. The comparison of prescribed rotations with fresh sampling makes the
distinction concrete: rotations improved several starting bodies, but fresh sampling reached the
larger maximum in each of the four comparisons. An instruction merely to find an improvement
could have returned a successful example while leaving the allocation question unanswered.
Here the control determines what we learn from the computation.
    The same issue occurs in more routine work. A retained code-repair example included
a regression test that passed even when the original sign defect was restored. Testing the
test against that defect revealed what an ordinary successful run did not establish. Such
checks are useful because they target a particular possible mistake, rather than adding another
undifferentiated approval of the result.

13.3    What still requires a reader
Mathematical exposition introduces a further difficulty. An agent can recover the meaning of a
dense paragraph and judge it correct while a human reader has to stop repeatedly to decipher
it. The intended reader of this thesis knows smooth symplectic geometry, but still needs an
introduction to the nonsmooth variational problem and its finite reduction. A later definition
does not remove the effort spent reading an earlier sentence that needed it.
    This makes reading feedback valuable even when individual calculations have been checked.
The useful feedback identifies where an explanation ceases to work: a missing implication,
an unfamiliar object used before its definition, or a paragraph whose contribution remains
unclear. Whether an agent can predict that feedback is itself an empirical question. Its ability
to summarize a chapter does not establish that the chapter is comfortable to read. Nor does
an automated check decide whether a mathematically correct side result deserves the space it
occupies. These judgments concern the reader and the purpose of the thesis.

13.4    A connection with training on verifiable outcomes
There is a suggestive connection to reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. Guo
and collaborators trained DeepSeek-R1-Zero using rule-based accuracy and format rewards;
mathematical answers and code test outcomes supplied checkable feedback. Their later DeepSeek-
R1 combined reinforcement learning with supervised fine-tuning and also used model-based
rewards for general tasks [GYZ+25]. This is evidence that outcome feedback can shape learned
problem-solving behavior, not that every useful judgment has a reliable automatic reward.
    An outcome supplied in an agent’s task context is not a training reward: the task does
not thereby update the model’s parameters. The practical connection is that both require a
criterion aligned with the intended achievement. In the HKO example, the finite conditions that
could be checked were themselves discovered during the research task. They were not a test
suite supplied in advance. My experience motivates using agents for work with clear outcomes
and checking whether the returned evidence actually supports them. It does not isolate the
contribution of a particular training method or measure the time this project would have taken
without AI.

14    Conclusion
Is the HKO body a local maximum of the systolic ratio among convex bodies? Our main theorem
gives a partial answer: it is a local maximum
                                        √        among ten-facet polytopes. Every sufficiently
nearby body has ratio at most (3 + 5)/5, and equality occurs precisely under translations,
positive dilations and linear symplectic maps. The restriction to ten facets is essential to the
coordinate neighborhood used in the proof.
    The argument also gives a useful way to work at a singular optimization problem. One can
continue feasible choices and use their values as upper bounds, without continuing stationary
optimizers. At HKO, finitely many such bounds suffice: after the symmetry directions are
removed, their derivatives force a strict decrease in every direction. The algebraic calculation
establishes the finite conditions, while the geometric proof identifies the upper bounds and
relates their coordinates to nearby bodies.
    The empirical work approached the problem from another direction. It used geometric
measurements and standard data-science methods to look for patterns that might lead back to
mathematical explanations. We found patterns below systolic ratio one and confirmed some of
their usefulness on fresh samples, but did not establish an extension to ratios above one. A
geometric calculation expresses the ridge-area measurement on products through edge lengths
and widths of the factors. It clarifies what the descriptor measures without explaining its
association with capacity. Turning that association into a theorem remains unfinished, and the
exploratory samples produced no new ratio above one.
    We implemented, optimized and extended two methods for computing capacity: the Haim–
Kislev quadratic program and the Chaidez–Hutchings flow graph. The expanded variational
proof explains why the first problem is finite; the second method gives an alternative for
cross-checking computations. For products of planar polygons, we proved reductions that make
the finite search smaller. An exhaustive exact calculation then proves the separate theorem
giving the capacity throughout the relative-rotation family of two regular pentagons, with the
HKO body a global maximizer of the systolic ratio within that family.
    Two directions remain particularly close to the questions studied here. First, the local
theorem could be tested against deformations that change the facet count. Adding or splitting
facets leaves the forty-dimensional chart used in the proof, so it requires a new argument.
Second, the statistical associations invite geometric interpretation. A useful next conjecture
would explain an association on a clearly specified family and survive attempts to break it by
changing the sampling distribution.
    The results therefore give a local theorem around the known counterexample, finite methods
for investigating nearby and sampled polytopes, and measured successes and failures of several
search ideas. The larger question of how capacity and volume constrain one another remains
open beyond these families and neighborhoods.

def cos_theta():
    return lift(1 - t**2) / lift(1 + t**2)

def sin_theta():
    return lift(2 * t) / lift(1 + t**2)

def rotate(point):
    x, y = point
    c, s = cos_theta(), sin_theta()
    return (c * x - s * y, s * x + c * y)

def pentagon_normals():
    return [(lift(cos_units(5 + 4*k)),
             lift(sin_units(5 + 4*k))) for k in range(5)]

def dual_vertices():
    height = lift(cos_units(2)) # cos(pi/5)
    normals = pentagon_normals()
    vertices = []
    for x, y in normals:
        vertices.append((x / height, y / height, 0, 0))
    for normal in normals:
        x, y = rotate(normal)
        vertices.append((0, 0, x / height, y / height))
    return vertices

def omega(u, v):
    return (u[0] * v[2] - u[2] * v[0]
            + u[1] * v[3] - u[3] * v[1])

def kkt_matrix(sigma):
    m = len(sigma)
    mat = Matrix(F, m + 5, m + 5, 0)
    rhs = vector(F, [0] * (m + 5))
    for i in range(m):
        for j in range(i + 1, m):
            value = omega(DUALS[sigma[i]], DUALS[sigma[j]])
            mat[i, j] = value
            mat[j, i] = value


                                                  75
    for i in range(m):
        for d in range(4):
            value = DUALS[sigma[i]][d]
            mat[i, m + d] = value
            mat[m + d, i] = value
        mat[i, m + 4] = 1
        mat[m + 4, i] = 1
    rhs[m + 4] = 1
    return mat, rhs

def q_value(sigma, beta):
    q = 0
    for i in range(1, len(sigma)):
        for j in range(i):
            q += (beta[i] * beta[j]
                  * omega(DUALS[sigma[j]], DUALS[sigma[i]]))
    return reduced(q)

def adjacent_same_factor(a, b):
    return (a - b) % 5 in {1, 4}

def blocks(facets):
    result = [(i,) for i in facets]
    for i, j in combinations(facets, 2):
        if adjacent_same_factor(i % 5, j % 5):
            result.append((i, j))
            result.append((j, i))
    return result

def non_overlapping(selection):
    used = set()
    for block in selection:
        if used & set(block):
            return False
        used.update(block)
    return True

def non_overlapping_selections(block_list, k):
    for selection in combinations(block_list, k):
        if non_overlapping(selection):
            yield selection

Exact sign cells and transition pruning
For a rational function in K(t), Sage isolates every numerator and denominator root in the
open half-domain. The resulting cells have constant sign, so one exact algebraic midpoint
decides each cell. The endpoint label retained in each certificate is diagnostic only; endpoints
are handled by the continuity argument above. Transition pruning uses the open-cell status
and checks that every mixed transition has no interior sign cut.

def real_roots_in_open_half_domain(poly):
    roots = []
    for root, multiplicity in poly.change_ring(AA).roots():
        root = AA(root)
        if AA(0) < root < HALF_DOMAIN_ENDPOINT:
            roots.append(root)
    return sorted(set(roots))

def sign_certificate(expr):
    expr = reduced(expr)
    numerator = expr.numerator()
    denominator = expr.denominator()
    if numerator == 0:
        return SignCertificate("zero", 0, "zero")
    cuts = sorted(set(
        real_roots_in_open_half_domain(numerator)
        + real_roots_in_open_half_domain(denominator)))
    points = [AA(0)] + cuts + [HALF_DOMAIN_ENDPOINT]
    num_aa = aa_polynomial(numerator)
    den_aa = aa_polynomial(denominator)
    signs = []
    for left, right in zip(points, points[1:]):
        sample = (left + right) / 2
        signs.append((num_aa(sample) / den_aa(sample)).sign())
    endpoint = endpoint_label(num_aa, den_aa)
    if all(sign > 0 for sign in signs):
        return SignCertificate("positive_open", len(cuts), endpoint)
    if all(sign < 0 for sign in signs):
        return SignCertificate("negative_open", len(cuts), endpoint)
    return SignCertificate("mixed_open", len(cuts), endpoint)

def open_domain_cells(expressions):
    cuts = []
    for expr in expressions:
        expr = reduced(expr)
        cuts += real_roots_in_open_half_domain(expr.numerator())
        cuts += real_roots_in_open_half_domain(expr.denominator())
    points = [AA(0)] + sorted(set(cuts))
    points += [HALF_DOMAIN_ENDPOINT]
    return [(a + b) / 2 for a, b in zip(points, points[1:])]

def facet_intersection_nonempty(i, j):
    if i == j:
        return True
    if (i < 5) != (j < 5):
        return True


                                              77
    return adjacent_same_factor(i % 5, j % 5)

def transition_table_open():
    table = {}
    for i in range(10):
        for j in range(10):
            cert = sign_certificate(omega(DUALS[i], DUALS[j]))
            if (i < 5) == (j < 5):
                 assert cert.status == "zero"
                 table[i, j] = facet_intersection_nonempty(i, j)
            else:
                 assert cert.status in {
                     "positive_open", "negative_open"
                 }
                 assert cert.interior_cut_count == 0
                 table[i, j] = (facet_intersection_nonempty(i, j)
                                and cert.status == "positive_open")
    return table

def transition_pruned_sigmas_open():
    table = transition_table_open()
    sigmas = []
    for k in (2, 3):
        for sigma in enumerate_k_bounce_sigmas(k):
            edges = zip(sigma, sigma[1:] + sigma[:1])
            if all(table[i, j] for i, j in edges):
                sigmas.append(sigma)
    return sigmas

Fail-closed branch classification
The classifier solves the displayed KKT equations over K(t). It accepts an identically zero
action gap as a tie; otherwise, on every cell where all weights and Qσ are positive, it requires a
strictly positive action gap. A singular or nonpositive-gap case not covered by a proved exit is
sent to manual review. The full loop rejects that status. For readability, status(...) below
abbreviates the executable’s result constructor.

ACCEPTED_STATUSES = {
    "no_kkt_solution", "singular_kkt_forced_zero_beta",
    "zero_q_identity", "zero_gap_identity",
    "not_feasible_on_open_domain",
    "strict_gap_positive_on_feasible_open_domain",
}

def forced_zero_weight(beta, kernel):
    for i in range(len(beta)):
        if beta[i] == 0 and all(reduced(v[i]) == 0 for v in kernel):
            return True
    return False

def classify_sigma(sigma, min_action):
    mat, rhs = kkt_matrix(sigma)
    try:
         solution = mat.solve_right(rhs)
    except ValueError:
         augmented = mat.augment(rhs.column())
         if augmented.rank() > mat.rank():


                                               78
              return status("no_kkt_solution")
          return status("requires_manual_review")

      beta = [reduced(solution[i]) for i in range(len(sigma))]
      q = q_value(sigma, beta)
      kernel = None
      if q == 0:
          kernel = mat.right_kernel().basis()
          if kernel:
              if forced_zero_weight(beta, kernel):
                  return status("singular_kkt_forced_zero_beta")
              return status("requires_manual_review")
          return status("zero_q_identity")

      gap = reduced(1 / (2 * q) - min_action)
      if gap == 0:
          return status("zero_gap_identity")

      feasible_cells = 0
      for sample in open_domain_cells(tuple(beta) + (q, gap)):
          feasible = all(sign_at(b, sample) > 0 for b in beta)
          feasible = feasible and sign_at(q, sample) > 0
          if feasible:
              feasible_cells += 1
              if sign_at(gap, sample) <= 0:
                  return status("requires_manual_review")

      if feasible_cells == 0:
          if kernel is None:
              kernel = mat.right_kernel().basis()
          if kernel:
              if forced_zero_weight(beta, kernel):
                  return status("singular_kkt_forced_zero_beta")
              return status("requires_manual_review")
          return status("not_feasible_on_open_domain")
      return status("strict_gap_positive_on_feasible_open_domain")

for sigma in transition_pruned_sigmas_open():
    classification = classify_sigma(sigma, minimum_action())
    assert classification.status in ACCEPTED_STATUSES
