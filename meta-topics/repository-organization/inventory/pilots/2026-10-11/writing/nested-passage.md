# From a ridge-area association to a selection rule

Can a geometric measurement identify bodies with large systolic ratio before their capacities are computed? The retained sample suggests that the symplectic areas of two-dimensional faces are useful for this purpose. It comprises 4,096 generic four-dimensional polytopes with 5–12 facets and 10,240 Lagrangian polygon products with factor side counts $3\leq k\leq m\leq6$, equally allocated within each kind of group. Facet normals were sampled uniformly on the relevant spheres and support heights uniformly in $[0.8,1.2)$, accepting only bounded bodies with all proposed facets present. The observation therefore concerns these admission-conditioned distributions, rather than an unspecified population of convex bodies.

The target is the systolic ratio
\[
\operatorname{sys}(K)=\frac{c_{\mathrm{EHZ}}(K)^2}{2\operatorname{vol}_4(K)}.
\]
For the empirical results below, $\widehat{\operatorname{sys}}$ denotes the recorded numerical ratio. Later reevaluation supplies certified capacity intervals for 14,335 of the 14,336 original bodies, but does not certify their floating-point volumes or retrospectively turn the recorded ratios into exact values.

A two-dimensional face is called a ridge. Its symplectic area is the absolute value of the integral of $\omega_0=dq_1\wedge dp_1+dq_2\wedge dp_2$ over that face. This can vanish even when the Euclidean area is positive. Define
\[
R(K)=\frac{\sum_F\left|\int_F\omega_0\right|}
{\sqrt{\operatorname{vol}_4(K)}}.
\]
Taking the absolute value on each face prevents cancellation between faces; dividing by the square root of volume removes common scale. Both $R$ and the systolic ratio are also unchanged by translations and linear symplectic maps.

In the broad sample, smaller $R$ accompanies larger numerical systolic ratio. The pooled Spearman correlation is approximately $-0.938$, whereas the Pearson correlation is only $-0.205$: the striking relation concerns ordering, rather than a linear dependence of values. Inverse rank association also occurs within every fixed facet-count or factor-side-count group, so it does not arise solely from pooling different groups. Nevertheless, restricting the original sample to its lowest ten percent by $R$ within groups weakens the pooled correlation of within-group ranks from $-0.944$ to $-0.222$. The broad ordering cannot simply be assumed to persist among the most promising bodies.

For Lagrangian products the measurement has a concrete geometric meaning. Put a polygon $P$ in the $q$-plane and $Q$ in the $p$-plane, with cyclic edge vectors $a_i,b_j$ and areas $A_P,A_Q$. The factor faces have zero symplectic area, while the mixed rectangular face associated with two edges has area $|a_i\cdot b_j|$. Hence
\[
R(P\times_LQ)=\frac{\sum_{i,j}|a_i\cdot b_j|}{\sqrt{A_PA_Q}}.
\]
This turns a statistical descriptor into a quantity that can be followed exactly along a rotation family.

For centered regular pentagons of circumradius one, let $K_\theta=P_5\times_L\operatorname{Rot}(\theta)P_5$ and $d=\operatorname{dist}(\theta,(\pi/5)\mathbb Z)\in[0,\pi/10]$. The edge formula gives $R(K_\theta)=16\sin(\pi/5)\cos d$. Combining it with the proved capacity profile yields
\[
\operatorname{sys}(K_\theta)R(K_\theta)^2=16(3+\sqrt5).
\]
Thus decreasing $R$ and increasing the systolic ratio are equivalent within this family. This is an exact explanation of one inverse relation, not a fitted trend. Its scope matters: for equilateral triangle products, both $R$ and the exact systolic ratio strictly decrease with the reduced rotation angle. Moreover, polygon products satisfy $R\geq8$, and the aligned cube attains this minimum with systolic ratio $1/2$; the pentagon HKO body has larger $R$ and ratio greater than one. Specified triangle–hexagon and square–square paths likewise show numerical decreases in both quantities at every retained step. These comparisons rule out treating ridge minimization as a universal improvement direction, without erasing its value for population selection.

That weaker task can be tested prospectively. In a fresh pool of 100,000 products, selecting the ten lowest-$R$ bodies in each side-count group before capacity evaluation gave mean numerical ratio $0.626$, against $0.316$ for equally allocated, disjoint same-pool controls chosen without capacity values. The candidate count is not an evaluation count: the packet evaluated a 1,675-body union across its selection and control sets. A separate 100,000-product pool tested an additional descriptor, $C=\max_F A_\omega(F)/\sum_F A_\omega(F)$, the largest ridge's share of total symplectic area. Within the lowest one percent by $R$ per group, the lower-$C$ half exceeded the upper half in mean ratio by $0.034$ overall, with positive differences in eight of ten groups. This compares two already selected halves, rather than isolating a causal effect of concentration.

Finally, the frozen two-stage rule was tested on a changed source: common support height one, separate factor area normalization, and joint eligibility restrictions. Each of the $4\times6$ and $6\times6$ groups supplied 3,200 eligible candidates, sixteen ridge-selected bodies and sixteen controls. Controls were chosen by fixed hash order outside both this selection and a concurrent covariance selection. The ridge-selected means were $0.637$ versus $0.371$ for $4\times6$, and $0.708$ versus $0.523$ for $6\times6$. These finite comparisons show that the rule retained a mean advantage on this particular changed source. Neither generated-selection packet nor the transfer panel found a numerical ratio above one. One transfer seed and two groups establish neither source-independent stability nor a probability of finding a counterexample. The geometric gain is therefore specific: ridge area encodes capacity variation exactly in one family and helps select higher-ratio populations in named experiments, while explicit families prevent promoting that success to a general optimization principle.
