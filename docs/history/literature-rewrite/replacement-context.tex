\subsection{What remains after the counterexample}

Viterbo's conjecture proposed an isoperimetric principle for symplectic
capacities: if \(K\subset\mathbb R^{2n}\) is convex, then
\[
  c(K)^n\leq n!\operatorname{vol}_{2n}(K),
\]
with the ball as the expected equality model \cite{Viterbo2000}.  For the
Ekeland--Hofer--Zehnder capacity in dimension four this becomes
\[
  \operatorname{sys}(K)
  :=\frac{c_{\mathrm{EHZ}}(K)^2}{2\operatorname{vol}_4(K)}\leq 1.
\]
The ratio removes scale and is unchanged by translations and linear
symplectic maps.  It therefore asks about shape rather than size.

Haim--Kislev and Ostrover disproved the universal inequality.  Their
four-dimensional example is the Lagrangian product of two regular pentagons
in a suitable relative position, and it satisfies
\[
  \operatorname{sys}(K_{\mathrm{HKO}})
  =\frac{3+\sqrt5}{5}>1
\]
\cite[Theorem~1.3 and Proposition~1.4]{HaimKislevOstrover2026}.  The failure
of the proposed bound changes the question rather than ending it.  One can
still ask how large the ratio can be, which geometric classes obey the
inequality, and whether the known counterexample is stable under nearby
changes of shape.  The centrally symmetric problem is especially important:
on products of a centrally symmetric body and its polar, the capacity--volume
inequality is tied to Mahler's conjecture \cite{ArtsteinAvidanKarasevOstrover2014}.

There is also a precise embedding meaning to the capacity computed in this
thesis.  Abbondandolo, Edtmair and Kang proved that on every convex body in
\(\mathbb R^4\),
\[
  c_{\mathrm{EHZ}}=c_Z,
\]
where \(c_Z\) is the cylindrical capacity
\cite[Corollary~1]{AbbondandoloEdtmairKang2024}.  Thus an EHZ computation for
one of the four-dimensional convex bodies considered here also determines
the smallest symplectic cylinder into which it embeds.  The scope of this
corollary matters.  Their stronger dynamical theorem, asserting that every
least-action characteristic bounds a disk-like global surface of section,
assumes uniform convexity; the equality of capacities extends to all convex
bodies.  Neither result identifies the Gromov width with EHZ.  Indeed, the
HKO counterexample separates the Gromov width from EHZ, while EHZ and the
cylindrical capacity still agree.

\subsection{The product boundary and the local question}

The HKO example belongs to the special class
\(K\times Q\subset\mathbb R_q^2\times\mathbb R_p^2\) of Lagrangian products.
In that class the EHZ capacity can be read as the length of a shortest
generalized Minkowski billiard trajectory.  This connection has recently
produced sharp positive results close to the combinatorics explored in this
thesis.  Balitskiy, Mitrofanov and Polyanskii prove the Viterbo inequality
when one planar factor is an arbitrary quadrilateral, and also treat
triangular factors and affinely regular hexagonal factors
\cite[Theorem~1.2 and Sections~4--5]{BalitskiyMitrofanovPolyanskii2026}.
Because capacity is symmetric in the two factors, the conclusion applies
with either factor in that role.

These results sharply delimit a product search.  A triangular or
quadrilateral factor cannot yield a new violation while the body remains a
Lagrangian product.  They do not exclude a general four-dimensional
polytope, nor a rotation that mixes the \(q\)- and \(p\)-planes and destroys
the product structure.  The regular-pentagon case lies just beyond the
quadrilateral theorem and is the setting where the known violation occurs.
This makes pentagonal products a natural boundary family, not merely one
more low-facet test case.

The thesis studies a different but complementary boundary: perturbations of
the counterexample itself.  Its main theorem says that
\(K_{\mathrm{HKO}}\) is a local maximum of the systolic ratio among nearby
polytopes with the same ten labelled facets, after quotienting by
translations, positive dilations and linear symplectic transformations.
This is a fixed-facet statement.  It does not cover perturbations in which
facets appear or disappear, and it does not assert local maximality among all
nearby convex bodies.

There is a smooth precedent for asking such a local question.  Zoll contact
forms, for which all Reeb trajectories are closed with the same minimal
period, locally maximize the contact systolic ratio
\cite[Theorem~1]{AbbondandoloBenedetti2023}.  The HKO body has corners and a
ratio strictly larger than the ball's, so that theorem does not settle its
behavior; it instead explains why the organization of minimizing
characteristics is relevant to local capacity--volume extremality.

That distinction is also visible in recent work on nonsmooth Zoll-type
dynamics.  Haim--Kislev studies how EHZ capacity behaves when a convex body
is cut by a hyperplane \cite{HaimKislev2025Zoll}.  For the HKO pentagon
product, Proposition~1.13 proves additivity for specified cut directions
near the boundary, while Example~1.12 reports broader numerical evidence
and leaves its generalized-Zoll status open.  Hyperplane cuts change the
facet structure; the theorem of this thesis instead varies the existing
ten-facet presentation.  The two viewpoints therefore concern adjacent but
non-equivalent notions of local rigidity.

\subsection{Why finite capacity methods are central here}

On a smooth convex boundary, \(c_{\mathrm{EHZ}}\) is the least action of a
closed characteristic.  On a polytope the analogous generalized
characteristics meet faces and corners, but the relevant optimization can
still be made finite.  Haim--Kislev expresses the EHZ capacity of a convex
polytope as an optimization over ordered facet data and nonnegative weights
\cite[Theorem~1.1]{HaimKislev2019}.  This quadratic-program description is
the common input to the thesis's exact capacity computations, its
product-specific reductions and the upper functions used in the HKO local
certificate.

A second route comes from combinatorial Reeb dynamics.  Chaidez and
Hutchings associate combinatorial orbits of a four-dimensional convex
polytope with Reeb orbits on smoothings and use the correspondence for
computer-assisted searches \cite{ChaidezHutchings2021}.  The flow method in
this thesis adapts that geometric viewpoint to an exact finite search under
explicit genericity and input hypotheses.  The two approaches answer
different finite questions: the quadratic program compares global action
values, whereas the flow construction follows passages between facets.  Their
agreement on shared inputs is consequently a substantive cross-check, not a
second notation for the same calculation.

These methods support two further parts of the thesis.  First, for
\(P_5\times R(\theta)P_5\) they yield the complete analytic profile
\[
  \operatorname{sys}\bigl(P_5\times R(\theta)P_5\bigr)
  =\frac{5+2\sqrt5}{10}\sec^2 d(\theta),
\]
where \(d(\theta)\) is the distance to the nearest multiple of
\(\pi/5\).  The HKO position is the maximum within this one-parameter
family.  This converts an isolated counterexample into a controlled
geometric deformation, without claiming a classification of general
pentagon products.  Second, the exploratory data-science work uses computed
capacities to look for reproducible patterns and then asks whether those
patterns admit geometric explanations.  Its negative searches and
subthreshold relations are evidence about the sampled families, not new
universal inequalities.  Together, the fixed-facet theorem, the finite
algorithms, the rotation profile and the exploratory search address the same
post-counterexample problem at different scales: which features of a convex
body make its shortest symplectic trajectory large relative to volume?
