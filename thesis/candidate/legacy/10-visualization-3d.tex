\section{Three-Dimensional Visualization}
\label{sec:visualization-3d}

A four-dimensional polytope cannot be drawn directly, but its boundary can be
viewed in three dimensions after choosing a projection.  For a polytope
\(K\subset\mathbb R^4\) with the origin in its interior, we first apply the
radial map
\[
  \partial K\longrightarrow S^3,
  \qquad x\longmapsto \frac{x}{\lVert x\rVert},
\]
and then stereographically project \(S^3\) from the north pole
\(e_4=(0,0,0,1)\) to \(\mathbb R^3\).  The viewer samples projected edges and
Reeb segments along great-circle arcs.  Its coloured two-face interiors are
interpolated spherical meshes, included to make the boundary structure easier
to see rather than to preserve metric geometry.  The stereographic projection
also distorts scale and clips arcs close to its pole.

Figure~\ref{fig:visualization-projections} shows projected two-faces of the
four-dimensional cube and the projected one-skeleton of the HKO polytope
\(K_{\mathrm{HKO}}\) from Section~\ref{sec:hko-local-maximum} with one
numerically recovered minimum-action generalized Reeb orbit.  The producer
exports only recovered orbits passing numerical closure and containment checks
within fixed tolerances.  The orbit shown is therefore a computed example, not
a proof of a capacity statement.

The interactive views were also explored as a possible source of geometric
patterns for our exploratory search.  In practice, passing from four dimensions to a
three-dimensional projection discarded too much visible Reeb-geometric
information, and the impressions changed with the projection pole.  We found
no visual pattern that gave a reliable hypothesis or candidate-selection rule.
For this exploration, the useful outcome is therefore limited to the explanatory role illustrated
above.
