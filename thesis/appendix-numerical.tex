\section{Numerical Implementation}\label{app:numerical}
\label{app:general-approach}

The algorithms in Section~\ref{sec:algorithms} are stated for
exact real arithmetic: predicates such as $\beta_i > 0$ and
$N^\top \beta = 0$ are decided exactly, and the KKT system is
solved without numerical error.
A floating-point implementation must control two kinds of error:
discrete decisions (combinatorial data, predicate evaluations)
may give wrong answers, and continuous quantities (KKT solutions)
accumulate rounding error.
We address these as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Rational input polytopes.}
  We restrict to polytopes with rational halfspace data
  $n_i \in \mathbb{Q}^4$, $h_i \in \mathbb{Q}$.
  This makes vertex enumeration, vertex--facet incidence,
  and symplectic signs $\operatorname{sign}(\omega_0(n_i, n_k))$
  exactly computable over~$\mathbb{Q}$
  (Section~\ref{app:polytope-combinatorics}).
  These exact quantities are stored alongside the
  floating-point polytope, so that rounding errors
  cannot affect discrete decisions.

\item \emph{Double-precision floating-point for continuous computation.}
  The KKT system and SVD decomposition are solved in
  \texttt{f64} arithmetic for performance.
  Rounding errors in continuous quantities are controlled
  by the strategies below.

\item \emph{Loose cutoffs ignore rounding.}
  Checks of the form ``$x > \varepsilon$'' with a
  tolerance~$\varepsilon$ much larger than machine precision
  are unaffected by rounding: the gap between the exact
  and rounded values is negligible compared to~$\varepsilon$.
  The numerical robustness conditions
  (Definition~\ref{def:numerically-robust}) formalise this.

\item \emph{Three-valued predicates for sharp cutoffs.}
  A sharp predicate such as $\beta_i > 0$ cannot be decided
  reliably from floating-point data alone.
  Instead of guessing, the implementation returns one of three
  verdicts: $\textsc{true}$, $\textsc{false}$, or
  $\textsc{unknown}$, deferring the uncertain cases to
  downstream logic.

\item \emph{Accumulation resolves most uncertain cases.}
  The algorithm tracks all candidates
  $(S, \sigma, \hat\beta, \hat Q)$ with their certainty
  status.
  Since we seek the \emph{maximum}~$Q$ over admissible
  candidates, uncertain candidates below the highest
  certified~$Q$ are irrelevant regardless of their true
  admissibility.
  In practice, this resolves all uncertain cases in our
  experiments (Section~\ref{app:accumulated-q-max}).

\item \emph{Fallback for unresolved cases.}
  If uncertain candidates remain above the certified maximum,
  the implementation can either signal an error (for manual
  investigation) or re-solve only the uncertain candidates
  using exact rational arithmetic, informed by the
  floating-point $(S, \sigma)$ and approximate~$\hat\beta$.
  No exact~$Q$ computation is needed---only the admissibility
  predicate $\beta_i > 0$ must be resolved exactly.
\end{enumerate}

The following definition makes the soundness requirement precise.

\begin{definition}[Sound predicate]%
\label{def:sound-predicate}
A predicate~$\tilde P$ computed from floating-point data
is \emph{sound} if its definite verdicts are reliable:
$\textsc{true}$ and $\textsc{false}$ agree with exact
arithmetic, and $\textsc{unknown}$ means the numerical
evidence is inconclusive.
Formally:
\begin{align*}
  P \text{ true in exact arithmetic}
  &\;\Longrightarrow\;
  \tilde P \neq \textsc{false}, \\
  P \text{ false in exact arithmetic}
  &\;\Longrightarrow\;
  \tilde P \neq \textsc{true}.
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Impact on control flow]%
\label{rem:three-valued-control-flow}
The exact algorithm has two kinds of decision points:
\begin{description}
\item[Inclusion decisions] (``unless $P$, skip''):
  The exact rule ``if $\lnot P$, skip $(S, \sigma)$''
  becomes ``if $\tilde P = \textsc{false}$, skip.''
  When $\tilde P = \textsc{unknown}$, the pair is kept
  (conservatively), since the exact~$P$ might be true.
  This means the numerical algorithm searches at least as
  broadly as the exact algorithm.
\item[Maximum tracking] (``find the largest admissible $Q$''):
  An admissibility predicate $A(\beta)$ (e.g.\ $\beta_i > 0$
  for all~$i$) becomes three-valued.
  The algorithm returns:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item the largest~$Q$ where $\tilde A = \textsc{true}$
    (the \emph{certified value}, a lower bound on the
    true maximum),
  \item all~$Q$ values above the certified value where
    $\tilde A = \textsc{unknown}$
    (the true maximum may be among these).
  \end{itemize}
  Candidates with $\tilde A = \textsc{false}$ are provably
  inadmissible and discarded; uncertain candidates at or below
  the certified value are irrelevant (the certified value already
  dominates).
\end{description}
\end{remark}
