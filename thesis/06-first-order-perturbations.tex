\section{First-Order Variation in Facet Coordinates}
\label{sec:first-order-perturbations}

The two preceding algorithms answer a scalar question: what is
\(c_{\mathrm{EHZ}}(K)\) for one fixed polytope?  Local search and local
maximality ask a different question: how does the systolic ratio change when
the facets move?  The Haim--Kislev formula makes this question finite, but not
automatically smooth.  A fixed facet word may give a smooth branch, several
branches may exchange the optimum, or a branch may appear through a zero
dwell time or a singular constraint system.

This section develops the first-order calculation where the relevant
Haim--Kislev constrained maxima are nondegenerate.  This condition is
unrelated to the flow-graph regularity in the preceding section.  The chapter
also isolates a weaker object that remains useful at degenerate branch data:
a smooth feasible choice of Haim--Kislev
weights gives an upper function for the systolic ratio, whether or not those
weights remain optimal.  The distinction is important.  The search methods use
nondegenerate branch gradients as local models, whereas the HKO proof in the next
section uses selected feasible upper functions and does not differentiate a
complete family of optimizing branches.

\subsection{Row coordinates and the volume variation}
\label{subsec:first-order-row-chart}

Fix a labelled presentation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-order-row-chart}
  K(a)=\{x\in\mathbb R^4:
    \langle a_i,x\rangle\leq 1,\ i=1,\ldots,F\},
  \qquad
  a=(a_1,\ldots,a_F).
\end{equation}
The vectors \(a_i\) are the dual vertices used in
Section~\ref{sec:quadratic-program-algorithm-hk2019}.  A row perturbation is a
tuple \(h=(h_1,\ldots,h_F)\in(\mathbb R^4)^F\), and we write
\(a(t)=a+th\).  Throughout this section we work in a neighborhood in which
\(K(a)\) remains a bounded full-dimensional polytope, the listed rows remain
irredundant, and the same simple face combinatorics persists.  In particular,
\(V(a)>0\), and the Haim--Kislev formula applies.  These are open conditions at
a simple labelled polytope.  They are local-chart hypotheses, not assertions
about every halfspace presentation.

For the facet \(F_i\), let \(S_i\) be its three-dimensional Euclidean volume
and let \(\bar x_i\) be its centroid.  The volume derivative has a particularly
simple expression in row coordinates.

\begin{proposition}[Volume first variation]
\label{prop:first-order-volume-variation}
In the stable simple chart above, with
\(V(a)=\operatorname{vol}_4(K(a))\),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-order-volume-variation}
  DV(a)[h]
  =-
  \sum_{i=1}^F
    \frac{S_i}{\lVert a_i\rVert}
    \langle \bar x_i,h_i\rangle.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
At a point \(x\in F_i\), differentiating the moving hyperplane equation
\(\langle a_i(t),x(t)\rangle=1\) gives
\[
  \langle a_i,\dot x\rangle=-\langle h_i,x\rangle.
\]
Thus the outward normal velocity of this facet is
\(-\langle h_i,x\rangle/\lVert a_i\rVert\).  The first variation of volume is
the integral of the outward normal velocity over the boundary.  Integrating on
each facet and using
\(\int_{F_i}x\,dS=S_i\bar x_i\) gives
\eqref{eq:first-order-volume-variation}.  Intersections of facets have
three-dimensional measure zero and do not contribute to the boundary
integral.
\end{proof}

The formula displays both parts of a row perturbation at once: changing
\(\lVert a_i\rVert\) moves the supporting hyperplane, while changing its
direction tilts that hyperplane.  It is also convenient computationally,
because it needs only facet volumes and centroids once the incidence data are
known.

\subsection{Feasible sections and smooth maxima}
\label{subsec:first-order-hk-branches}

Fix an ordered word
\(\sigma=(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m)\) with distinct facets.  Write
\[
  C_\sigma(a)\beta
  =
  \left(
    \sum_{k=1}^m\beta_k a_{\sigma_k},
    \sum_{k=1}^m\beta_k
  \right),
  \qquad e=(0,0,0,0,1),
\]
so that the Haim--Kislev feasibility equations are
\(C_\sigma(a)\beta=e\) and \(\beta\geq0\).  We use the quadratic objective
from \eqref{eq:quadratic-program-algorithm-hk2019-q}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-order-fixed-word-q}
  Q_\sigma(a,\beta)
  =
  \sum_{1\leq j<k\leq m}
    \beta_j\beta_k\,
    \omega_0(a_{\sigma_j},a_{\sigma_k}).
\end{equation}
For later differentiation, its explicit row variation at fixed \(\beta\) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-order-explicit-q-variation}
\begin{split}
  D_aQ_\sigma(a,\beta)[h]
  ={}&\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq m}\beta_j\beta_k
  \bigl(
    \omega_0(h_{\sigma_j},a_{\sigma_k})
    +\omega_0(a_{\sigma_j},h_{\sigma_k})
  \bigr).
\end{split}
\end{equation}

First consider any smooth feasible choice, without imposing stationarity.

\begin{lemma}[Feasible-section upper function]
\label{lem:first-order-feasible-section-upper-function}
Suppose that \(\beta(a)\) is smooth near \(a_0\),
\[
  C_\sigma(a)\beta(a)=e,
  \qquad \beta(a)>0,
  \qquad Q_\sigma(a,\beta(a))>0.
\]
Define
\[
  A_\sigma(a)=\frac{1}{2Q_\sigma(a,\beta(a))},
  \qquad
  U_\sigma(a)=\frac{A_\sigma(a)^2}{2V(a)}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-order-upper-function}
  \operatorname{sys}(K(a))\leq U_\sigma(a)
\end{equation}
throughout this neighborhood.  If the chosen weights attain the global
Haim--Kislev maximum at \(a_0\), then equality holds in
\eqref{eq:first-order-upper-function} at \(a_0\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The chosen \(\beta(a)\) is feasible for the global maximum defining
\(Q_{\max}(a)\).  Hence
\(Q_{\max}(a)\geq Q_\sigma(a,\beta(a))\).  The Haim--Kislev formula gives
\[
  c_{\mathrm{EHZ}}(K(a))
  =\frac{1}{2Q_{\max}(a)}
  \leq A_\sigma(a).
\]
Squaring and dividing by the positive number \(2V(a)\) proves the upper
bound.  A globally maximizing choice gives equality at the base point.
\end{proof}

The lemma requires no KKT system.  For example, if
\(C_\sigma(a_0)\) has rank five and \(\beta_0>0\) is feasible, choose an
invertible five-column minor and hold the other \(m-5\) coordinates of
\(\beta\) fixed.  Solving the five feasibility equations gives a smooth local
section through \(\beta_0\); positivity persists after shrinking the
neighborhood.  This elementary construction is the one used for the singular
seven-facet data in the HKO certificate.

At a nondegenerate constrained maximum, the KKT equations instead choose a canonical
optimizing branch and remove the derivative of \(\beta\) from the value
derivative.  Let \(H_\sigma(a)\) denote the Hessian of
\(Q_\sigma(a,\cdot)\) with respect to \(\beta\).

\begin{definition}[Nondegenerate Haim--Kislev branch]
\label{def:first-order-regular-hk-branch}
A feasible pair \((\sigma,\beta_0)\) at \(a_0\) is a \emph{nondegenerate branch}
if
\begin{enumerate}
\item \(C_\sigma(a_0)\) has rank five;
\item \(\beta_0>0\) and \(Q_\sigma(a_0,\beta_0)>0\);
\item \(\beta_0\) is a constrained critical point of
  \(Q_\sigma(a_0,\cdot)\); and
\item \(H_\sigma(a_0)\) is negative definite on
  \(\ker C_\sigma(a_0)\).
\end{enumerate}
For \(m=5\), the kernel is zero and the last condition is vacuous.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Derivative of a nondegenerate branch]
\label{prop:first-order-regular-branch}
Let \((\sigma,\beta_0)\) be a nondegenerate branch at \(a_0\).  Then, after
shrinking the row chart, there is a unique smooth constrained maximizer
\(\beta_\sigma(a)>0\) near \(\beta_0\).  Put
\[
  q_\sigma(a)=Q_\sigma(a,\beta_\sigma(a)),
  \qquad
  U_\sigma(a)=
  \frac{1}{2V(a)}\left(\frac{1}{2q_\sigma(a)}\right)^2.
\]
If \(\lambda_\sigma\in\mathbb R^5\) is chosen so that the KKT equation is
\[
  D_\beta Q_\sigma(a_0,\beta_0)
  =C_\sigma(a_0)^{\mathsf T}\lambda_\sigma,
\]
then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-order-regular-q-derivative}
  Dq_\sigma(a_0)[h]
  =D_aQ_\sigma(a_0,\beta_0)[h]
   -\left\langle
      \lambda_\sigma,
      D_aC_\sigma(a_0)[h]\,\beta_0
    \right\rangle.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-order-regular-sys-derivative}
  \frac{DU_\sigma(a_0)[h]}{U_\sigma(a_0)}
  =-2\frac{Dq_\sigma(a_0)[h]}{q_\sigma(a_0)}
   -\frac{DV(a_0)[h]}{V(a_0)}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Full rank and negative definiteness make the saddle-point KKT matrix
nonsingular.  The implicit function theorem therefore gives a unique smooth
critical point near \(\beta_0\).  Negative definiteness and positivity are
open conditions, so this point remains the positive constrained maximum.

Differentiate
\(q_\sigma(a)=Q_\sigma(a,\beta_\sigma(a))\).  The term containing
\(D\beta_\sigma\) is
\[
  D_\beta Q_\sigma\,D\beta_\sigma
  =\lambda_\sigma^{\mathsf T}
    C_\sigma D\beta_\sigma.
\]
Differentiating
\(C_\sigma(a)\beta_\sigma(a)=e\) gives
\(C_\sigma D\beta_\sigma=-(D_aC_\sigma)\beta_\sigma\), which proves
\eqref{eq:first-order-regular-q-derivative}.  The logarithmic derivative of
\(U_\sigma=(2V)^{-1}(2q_\sigma)^{-2}\) is
\eqref{eq:first-order-regular-sys-derivative}.
\end{proof}

Equations~\eqref{eq:first-order-explicit-q-variation},
\eqref{eq:first-order-volume-variation}, and
\eqref{eq:first-order-regular-q-derivative} are the practical first-order
calculation.  They use the current rows, the word, its positive KKT weights and
multipliers, and facet volumes and centroids.  They do not require finite
differences of the full capacity computation.

\subsection{From branches to the systolic ratio}
\label{subsec:first-order-branch-envelope}

A nondegenerate branch always gives the upper function \(U_\sigma\), but it gives the
actual systolic ratio only while it realizes the global Haim--Kislev maximum.
This global step must not be inferred from KKT nondegeneracy alone.

Suppose that finitely many nondegenerate branches have fixed-word maxima
\(q_1,\ldots,q_r\) on a neighborhood of \(a_0\), and that they cover the
global finite problem there in the explicit sense
\[
  Q_{\max}(a)=\max_{1\leq j\leq r}q_j(a).
\]
For example, this identity follows from a complete retained word list and a
persistent value gap for every discarded word.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-order-regular-envelope}
  \operatorname{sys}(K(a))=\min_{1\leq j\leq r}U_j(a).
\end{equation}
If \(I(a_0)=\{j:U_j(a_0)=\operatorname{sys}(K(a_0))\}\), the one-sided
directional derivative is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-order-envelope-derivative}
  D^+\operatorname{sys}(a_0;h)
  =\min_{j\in I(a_0)}DU_j(a_0)[h].
\end{equation}
Indeed, this is the first-order expansion of a finite minimum of smooth
functions.  In particular, a unique active nondegenerate branch gives an ordinary
gradient.  At a tie of nondegenerate branches, the directional derivative is generally only the
lower envelope of the tied branch slopes.  We use this direct envelope
description rather than calling it a Clarke derivative.

The hypothesis preceding \eqref{eq:first-order-regular-envelope} contains real
mathematical work.  A list returned by one numerical solve, or a tolerance
window around its smallest actions, is not by itself a certificate of the
displayed identity.

\subsection{What the first-order model contributes to search}
\label{subsec:first-order-search-model}

On a nondegenerate branch patch with active branch rows
\(r_j=DU_j(a_0)\), a common first-order ascent direction in a chosen linear
slice \(S\) can be sought through the maximin problem
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-order-maximin-direction}
  \max_{h\in S,\ \lVert h\rVert\leq1}
  \min_{j\in I(a_0)} r_j(h).
\end{equation}
The slice removes directions generated by the systolic-ratio symmetries from
Definition~\ref{def:preliminaries-systolic-ratio-symmetries}.  The true ratio
is constant on the corresponding group orbits, so its derivative vanishes on
their tangent space; \(S\) is chosen as a complementary linear slice.  A
positive value in
\eqref{eq:first-order-maximin-direction} predicts improvement of every active
nondegenerate branch.  The prediction still has to be checked by recomputing the
capacity after a finite step.

At finite distance, currently inactive branches can matter before they are
active.  A useful semi-local model therefore retains their base gaps:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first-order-finite-gap-model}
  \min_{j\in W}
  \bigl(
    U_j(a_0)-\operatorname{sys}(K(a_0))+DU_j(a_0)[h]
  \bigr),
\end{equation}
where \(W\) is a chosen branch window.  Formula
\eqref{eq:first-order-finite-gap-model} explains why a single active gradient
can miss a nearby branch switch, but it is a prediction model rather than an
infinitesimal theorem.  Its reliability depends on the window containing the
branches that become relevant, on the branches remaining feasible, and on the
linearization error at the proposed step.

The retained search evidence uses this distinction conservatively.  On a
fixed panel of twelve random ten-facet starts, the current multi-direction
method accepted a positive move at each of eight configured trace iterations,
but all runs
stopped at their iteration cap and every endpoint scan still found another
tested improvement.  Thus the evidence supports finite-step progress and a
measured computation cost on that panel, not endpoint local maximality.  The
newer branch-window prediction work remains a development surface; it is not
used as thesis evidence here.  It should be reconsidered for this chapter only
after a reproducible result packet fixes the base-point panel, window policy,
finite radii, prediction error, and branch-miss rate.

\subsection{The non-generic boundary}
\label{subsec:first-order-nongeneric-boundary}

The nondegenerate calculation is deliberately not presented as a complete local
theory for arbitrary polytopes.  Four failures delimit its present scope.

\begin{itemize}
\item If a maximizing weight is zero, deleting that coordinate is valid for
  the capacity value at the base point.  It need not preserve the nearby
  slope: the longer word may acquire a positive weight on one side of the
  perturbation and define a different branch there.
\item If the constraint rank drops or the KKT matrix is singular, the implicit
  function theorem does not provide a unique smooth optimizing branch.  A
  continuum of optimizers may nevertheless have a well-defined value.
\item A tie is described by \eqref{eq:first-order-envelope-derivative} only
  when all relevant tied branches extend smoothly and the stated coverage
  and gap hypotheses hold.  Base-point branch enumeration alone does not prove
  this.
\item When facet labels become redundant or the face combinatorics changes,
  both the branch family and the smooth volume formula require a new local
  chart.  Hausdorff continuity of capacity and volume across that boundary
  does not imply differentiability there.
\end{itemize}

All constraints and objectives are semialgebraic.  A possible route to a fully
general one-sided theory would organize germs of feasible optimizing branches
by a finite decomposition of direction space.  Such a construction
would have to record support appearances, singular optimizer sets, and
dominance of every discarded germ.  This thesis does not prove that heavier
catalogue theorem.  The unresolved question is therefore not whether nondegenerate
branch gradients can be computed, but how to obtain a complete and economical
first-order object at an arbitrary non-generic polytope.

\subsection{Why the HKO certificate uses upper functions}
\label{subsec:first-order-hko-upper-functions}

The feasible-section lemma supplies a different first-order argument that does
not require the complete non-generic catalogue.  The following elementary
criterion is the logical bridge.

\begin{lemma}[Finite upper-function criterion]
\label{lem:first-order-finite-upper-function-criterion}
Let \(f\) be a function on a finite-dimensional linear space \(S\), and let
\(U_1,\ldots,U_N\) be smooth near \(0\) such that
\[
  f(s)\leq U_j(s),
  \qquad U_j(0)=f(0)
  \quad(j=1,\ldots,N).
\]
Put \(r_j=DU_j(0)\in S^*\).  Suppose that the rows \(r_j\) span \(S^*\) and
that there are numbers \(\lambda_j>0\) with
\[
  \sum_{j=1}^N\lambda_j=1,
  \qquad
  \sum_{j=1}^N\lambda_jr_j=0.
\]
Then \(f(s)<f(0)\) for every sufficiently small nonzero \(s\in S\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For every nonzero \(v\in S\), some \(r_j(v)\) is negative.  Otherwise all
rows would be nonnegative on \(v\), and their strictly positive convex
combination could vanish only if every \(r_j(v)=0\), contradicting that the
rows span \(S^*\).  By compactness of the unit sphere and finiteness of the
row set, there is a uniform \(\delta>0\) such that
\(\min_j r_j(v)\leq-\delta\) for every unit vector \(v\).  The smooth Taylor
remainders are uniformly \(O(\lVert s\rVert^2)\).  For sufficiently small
nonzero \(s\), choose a row with
\(r_j(s/\lVert s\rVert)\leq-\delta\); then
\[
  f(s)\leq U_j(s)
  \leq f(0)-\delta\lVert s\rVert+O(\lVert s\rVert^2)
  <f(0).
\]
\end{proof}

For HKO, \(S\) is a \(25\)-dimensional slice transverse to translations,
scaling, and linear symplectic transformations.  The exact certificate in the
next section constructs \(26\) positive feasible Haim--Kislev sections.  Their
upper functions touch the true systolic ratio at the HKO point.  The verifier
checks that their derivative rows span \(S^*\) and satisfy the positive convex
relation in Lemma~\ref{lem:first-order-finite-upper-function-criterion}.
This proves strict decrease in every transverse direction even though some of
the selected data have singular KKT systems.  No assertion that these
feasible sections remain optimizing branches nearby is needed.

\subsection{Implementation and validation status}
\label{subsec:first-order-validation}

The implementation evaluates the branch and volume formulas in dual-vertex
coordinates.  Regression tests compare the volume derivative with central
finite differences and compare a fixed-word capacity derivative with central
finite differences on known fixtures.  An exact rational implementation of
the capacity-gradient formula is also compared with the floating-point formula
on a rational simplex fixture.  These checks test the correspondence between
the formulas and their implementations; they do not certify branch coverage,
finite-step prediction, or local maximality.

For the HKO theorem, the evidence standard is stronger and different.  The
SageMath verifier checks the selected feasible sections and their derivative
rows exactly in the relevant algebraic number field.  The mathematical use of
those rows is Lemma~\ref{lem:first-order-finite-upper-function-criterion} and
the transverse-slice argument in the next section, not a numerical gradient
experiment.
