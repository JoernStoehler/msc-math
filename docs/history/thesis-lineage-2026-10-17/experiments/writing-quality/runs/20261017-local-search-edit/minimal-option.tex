\subsection{Competing branches in local search}
\label{subsec:black-box-datascience-finite-budget-optimization}

Increasing one currently minimizing branch can expose another as the new
minimum, as the lower-envelope model in
Section~\ref{subsec:first-order-search-model} explains. The
finite-gap model therefore retains nearby branches, and history-based
variants also retain previously active ones. A numerically chosen branch
window is not guaranteed to contain every relevant branch.

Retaining branches from earlier points gave the largest median
best-so-far numerical ratios in the comparison. Seven fixed
implementations shared the same 64 generic ten-facet starting bodies, a cap
of 128 full evaluations and a nominal one-second accumulated
evaluator-plus-optimizer budget:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\hline
Implementation & Median best ratio & Across-start $10$--$90\%$ range\\
\hline
Four-anchor history & .985 & .957--.999\\
History with transition prediction & .982 & .956--.994\\
Finite-gap model & .977 & .948--.995\\
Literal gradient & .881 & .790--.924\\
Safeguarded gradient & .855 & .780--.916\\
CMA-ES & .818 & .718--.869\\
Coordinate poll & .601 & .358--.787\\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The finite-gap method models branches near the current minimum. The two
history methods also retain earlier branches; one predicts additional
branches from transitions. CMA-ES~\cite{dsHansen2016} and coordinate polling
supply population and direct-search comparisons. These are rankings of
the fixed implementations under this allocation, not of optimally tuned
algorithm families. The comparison uses a heuristic floating-point objective,
rather than the certified capacity implementation of Section~\ref{sec:numerics}.
Its geometry and volume are floating-point computations. Appendix~\ref{app:ds-local-optimization} specifies the
available implementations, traces and budget interpretation. The ranges in the table
show variation across starting bodies; all 448 best-so-far ratios are below one.
