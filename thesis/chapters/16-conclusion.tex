\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The HKO body is a local maximum of the systolic ratio among ten-facet
polytopes. Every sufficiently nearby body in this space has ratio at most
$(3+\sqrt5)/5$, and equality occurs precisely under translations, positive
dilations and linear symplectic maps. This answers the local-improvement
question within the fixed-facet space. Deformations that introduce new
facets require a different local model.

A global result gives the same sharp bound for products of independently
linearly deformed regular pentagons. Its equality cases are positive dilates
of linear symplectic images of HKO. Together, the two theorems rule out
improvement by sufficiently small ten-facet perturbations and by arbitrary
deformations within this family of affinely regular products. The latter
can be large, while the former can destroy the product structure.

The placement of the factor planes matters even when the Euclidean product
shape is fixed. Every symplectic product of full-dimensional planar convex
bodies admits a capacity-increasing rigid rotation. The Lagrangian endpoint
can nevertheless have smaller capacity; in the triangle example the maximum
along the path lies strictly between the endpoints. Thus the familiar
symplectic and Lagrangian arrangements need not contain the best position
of a given product.

At HKO, the local proof continues feasible choices through a singular
optimization problem and uses their values as upper bounds, without
continuing stationary optimizers. Finitely many such bounds suffice: their
derivatives force a strict decrease in every nonzero direction transverse to the symmetries. The algebraic
calculation establishes the finite conditions, while the geometric proof
identifies the upper bounds and relates their coordinates to nearby bodies.

The finite methods also support computation beyond these proofs. The
Haim--Kislev formula gives a finite capacity problem for convex polytopes,
and the product reduction shows that some maximizing word uses at most three
facet directions from each planar factor. Its implementation supplies the
capacity evaluations used to investigate broader populations. Our flow-graph
construction uses the local passage geometry of Chaidez and Hutchings and
provides a second capacity algorithm under explicit regularity assumptions.

In those populations, symplectic ridge area gives a strong coarse predictive
signal for computed systolic ratios. Geometric selection raised mean
computed ratios in fresh samples. The pooled inverse rank association
weakens substantially when the original data are restricted within each sampling group to low ridge scores
or high computed ratios, with ranks recomputed in the retained groups.
Thus a strong ordering across the broad population becomes much weaker
among selected candidates. Restricting the range can itself attenuate
correlation, so this observation alone does not identify a geometric cause.

The balancing experiment goes further by changing the shapes. Applying
determinant-one maps that make each factor's vertex covariance isotropic
raises the mean numerical ratio from $0.342$ to $0.654$ in 320 paired
products, with 314 increases and six decreases. The pooled within-group
rank correlation weakens from $-0.929$ to $-0.249$. Affine changes in factor
shape therefore contribute materially to the broad association in this
sample. The intervention changes several geometric features together, so
it does not identify covariance imbalance as the sole cause or give a
universal improvement rule.

The residual relationship depends on the family. Relative rotations of
regular pentagon factors satisfy the inverse-square law
\eqref{eq:ds-pentagon-ridge-capacity}, whereas relative rotations of
equilateral-triangle factors give the same strict ordering by ridge sum and
systolic ratio, as proved in~\eqref{eq:ds-triangle-profile}. Ideal covariance
balancing makes triangles equilateral, which explains the opposite ordering
in that balanced family. These exact laws show that even the direction of
the relationship depends on geometric restrictions.

The local-search comparison supplies another practical conclusion. On the
same 64 starts under common evaluation limits, the four-anchor history
implementation attained median best ratio $0.985$, compared with $0.881$
for literal gradient ascent. Retaining previously active branches was useful
in this finite comparison. Endpoint tests nevertheless found ascent
directions missed by simpler probes, so high terminal values did not certify
local maxima. None of the exploratory searches produced a new numerical
ratio above one.

Two open questions follow directly. Does HKO remain locally maximal
when the facet count changes? Which geometric features explain the residual
ridge association after factor balancing, and how does that relation depend
on the sampling distribution? The
first asks how far the proved local extremality extends. The second asks
which capacity laws extend beyond the solved rotation families.
