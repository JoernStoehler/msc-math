\section[A Bounded Negative Data-Science Search]{A Bounded Negative\\Data-Science Search}\label{sec:black-box-datascience}

The preceding sections study the Haim--Kislev--Ostrover construction and its
local behavior.  This section records a different kind of evidence.  We ask
whether an ordinary data-science loop, applied to random polytopes and random
Lagrangian products, finds either a new polytope with systolic ratio larger than
\(1\) or a reproducible rule that should be used to look for one.

The conclusion is negative in a bounded sense.  In the retained random/product
table, none of the \(14336\) rows has systolic ratio larger than \(1\).  The
method packets applied to this table do recover structure in the already
computed values of the systolic ratio, but they do not validate a new source of
counterexamples.  A separate frozen scalar-filter run over generated
random-product candidates also stayed below the threshold.  A held-out
comparison of seven local optimizers on random ten-facet polytopes reached
values close to \(1\), but none above it.  Thus the result is not a theorem
that random or local search cannot succeed; it is evidence that these bounded
routes did not produce a new source of examples.
The contribution is a controlled negative benchmark: it separates explanation
of already computed values from evidence for a new way to find counterexamples.

\subsection{The retained random/product table}
\label{subsec:black-box-datascience-retained-random-product-table}

The active search surface has two row families.  The first consists of generic
random polytopes with \(F=5,\ldots,12\) facets, with \(512\) accepted samples
for each facet count.  The second consists of random Lagrangian products of
polygons, with \(1024\) accepted samples for each pair \(3\leq k\leq m\leq 6\)
of side counts.  Both producers use seed \(42\), height interval
\([0.8,1.2]\), and rejection until a valid polytope is produced.

This table is the main negative datum of the section.  It should be read with
its sampling contract.  The sample does not vary the seed, height interval,
facet-count range, product side-count range, or random model.  Therefore it
supports a finite-sample statement about the retained table, not a statement
about all random polytopes or all random Lagrangian products.

\subsection{Invariant descriptors and provenance}
\label{subsec:black-box-datascience-invariant-features-and-provenance}

For the statistical analysis, the numerical descriptors are invariant
quantities of the polytope: combinatorial quantities from the face lattice and
symplectic two-face area statistics normalized by the square root of the volume.
The construction history of a row is kept separately.  This distinction matters
because a model that predicts the systolic ratio from sampling provenance is
less interesting than a model that finds geometry.

The descriptor set is intentionally conservative.  It does not use raw
Euclidean representative coordinates or sign/cutoff features.  Those quantities
may still be useful in future work, but they need separate invariance and
boundary diagnostics before they can support the same interpretation.

\subsection{What counted as progress}
\label{subsec:black-box-datascience-result-types}

There are three different success levels.

First, the strongest outcome would be a validated new row with systolic ratio
larger than \(1\).  No row in the retained random/product table has this
property.

Second, a method could be a candidate proposer: it could freeze a rule that
selects new candidate polytopes before their systolic ratios are computed, and
the selected candidates could then be evaluated.  This is stronger than finding
an association inside a table whose target values are already known.

Third, a method can still be useful as an in-table diagnostic.  Such a method
may explain variation in the retained table, identify where the high tail is
concentrated, or suggest a future frozen rule.  It does not by itself produce a
new candidate source.

\subsection{Method outcomes}
\label{subsec:black-box-datascience-method-outcomes}

The direct scan found no row with systolic ratio larger than \(1\).  Descriptive
tail diagnostics found that the largest retained value was about \(0.863\), and
that the high tail is concentrated in particular product-heavy or high-facet
views.  These are overlapping views of the same retained rows, not independent
searches.

The model-based diagnostics show in-table predictability, not search success.
Invariant descriptors predicted the already computed systolic ratio much better
than provenance-only baselines.  Scalar association, projection, clustering,
anomaly, and shallow rule diagnostics likewise found structure in the retained
table.  The ridge symplectic-area descriptors were especially visible in the
scalar and rule-based diagnostics.  This helps interpret the sampled table, but
it is not yet a candidate-proposer claim: the ranked rows already had their
systolic ratios computed.

The generated-candidate follow-up is closer to the desired search interface.
In the current scalar-filter packet, the selection rules were frozen before
systolic ratios were computed.  The run generated \(100000\) random-product
candidates and then evaluated the candidates selected by the frozen rules
together with a baseline comparison set, \(1675\) rows in total.  No evaluated
candidate had systolic ratio larger than \(1\); the largest evaluated value was
about \(0.868\).  This supports the same bounded conclusion for that generator
and rule set, not a theorem about all scalar rules or all random-product
generators.

Mechanism diagnostics give empirical evidence about which ridge-area
explanations are currently most plausible.  Ridge symplectic-area descriptors
help explain high retained values, but not enough to produce near-threshold
candidates.  Purely combinatorial or product-bucket explanations are too crude.
Generated per-bucket low-ridge rules improved mean systolic ratio over matched
baselines, but selected maxima stayed below \(1\).  Some concentration splits
were found only after target values were known, so their proper role is to seed
a possible future frozen rule, not to serve as evidence that a valid proposer
already exists.

Older experiments on fixed-facet ascent and its endpoints remain historical
context.  The finite-budget comparison below is a
separately frozen experiment with disjoint tuning and held-out starts.  It
does not provide evidence for exhaustive search or local maximality.  The
selected-body screen that follows is separate again: it starts from a frozen
theory-selected panel, retains its own probes and controls, and supports only
the explicitly bounded local interpretation stated there.

behind the black-box summary.
